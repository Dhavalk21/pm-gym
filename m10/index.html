1Events and tracking plansDecide what to measure before you need the answer.8 min

The idea

Product analytics runs on events: records that something happened, like invoice_sent, with properties that describe it (amount, currency, platform). A tracking plan is the shared list of which events you track, what they're called and what they mean. Without one, every team names things differently and nobody trusts the numbers.

The framework

  1. Start from questions, not from "track everything": what decisions will this data inform?
  2. Name events consistently: object then action, past tense, like invoice_sent or trial_started.
  3. Write down a definition for each: when exactly does it fire, and on which platforms?
  4. Test before you release, and check after: a release can silently break tracking.

Real example

One product had Signup on web, sign_up on Android and user_registered on iOS, each firing at a slightly different moment. The "sign-up" chart added three different things together. A one-page tracking plan, and a week of clean-up, made the funnel trustworthy again.

Where it breaks

  • Collect only what you need. In the EU, GDPR and consent rules apply to tracking. Less data is also less to maintain.
  • Events show what people did, not why. Pair them with interviews (M5) to understand the reason.

Drill: better event name?

Which is the best name for the event fired when someone finishes paying?

2FunnelsFind the step where people give up.8 min

The idea

A funnel is a sequence of steps towards a goal, like visit, sign up, create first invoice, get paid. It shows what share of people make it from each step to the next, so you can see where most of them drop off.

The framework

  1. Define the steps and the time window: "within 7 days of sign-up".
  2. Look at step-to-step conversion, not just the overall rate.
  3. Fix the biggest drop that matters, then look again.
  4. Split the funnel by segment (platform, channel, new or returning) before drawing conclusions.

Real example

Ledgerly's funnel: 1,000 sign-ups, 620 connect a bank account, 300 send a first invoice, 240 get paid. The biggest drop is between connecting a bank account and sending an invoice. Interviews show why: people don't have a client's details at hand at sign-up. Letting them save a draft and come back later becomes the next experiment.

Where it breaks

  • Drop-off shows where, not why. Watch sessions or talk to users to find the reason.
  • Real journeys loop and skip steps. A strict funnel can undercount people who take another route.

Drill: which step first?

Visit to sign-up 20%, sign-up to setup 85%, setup to first use 30%, first use to repeat use 70%. Where would you dig first?

3Cohorts and retentionGroup people by when they started, then watch who stays.9 min

The idea

A cohort is a group of users who started in the same period, like everyone who signed up in the week of 3 March. Retention is the share of a cohort still active after one, two, four or twelve weeks. Comparing cohorts shows whether the product is getting better at keeping people, which an overall average can hide.

The framework

  1. Define "active" by the value moment, not just a login: sent an invoice, finished a lesson.
  2. Plot retention curves by cohort: share still active against weeks since start.
  3. Look for the curve to flatten. A flat tail means a group of people who keep getting value.
  4. Compare cohorts by start date, channel and first-week behaviour.

Real example

Facebook's early growth team compared cohorts of users who stayed with those who left. Chamath Palihapitiya, then its head of growth, described the pattern they found: people who connected with about seven friends within their first ten days tended to stay. That became the team's activation goal, and features like friend suggestions were built around it.

Where it breaks

  • Correlation isn't cause. Maybe engaged people add friends, not the other way round. Test it (M11) before betting the roadmap.
  • Small cohorts are noisy. Don't read much into a few dozen users.

Drill: read the curve

Retention for each new monthly cohort falls fast and keeps falling towards zero. What does it suggest?

4Diagnosing a metric dropIs it real? When? Where? Rate or mix?9 min

The idea

When a key number suddenly moves, the worst response is the first plausible story. A simple order of questions solves most cases quickly and stops you "fixing" something that wasn't broken.

The framework

  1. Is it real? Check tracking, data pipelines and definition changes. Compare with another source, like the database.
  2. When did it start, and what changed then? Releases, campaigns, outages, holidays.
  3. Where is it? Split by platform, version, country, channel, new or returning.
  4. Rate or mix? Did a group get worse, or did the mix of groups change?

Real example

The mix question catches even experts. In a famous 1973 study of graduate admissions at UC Berkeley, men were admitted at a higher rate overall. Department by department, women were admitted at a similar or slightly higher rate. Women had mostly applied to departments that admitted fewer people of any gender. This is called Simpson's paradox.

Where it breaks

  • Several causes can overlap. A small real drop can hide inside a big tracking problem.
  • Not every wiggle needs a story. Know your normal week-to-week noise before you raise the alarm.

Drill: what first?

Sign-ups dropped 30% overnight, right after a release. What do you check first?

Cases

One team that used cohort data to find what made people stay, one company whose metric definition misled its customers for two years.

Got it right

Facebook's "7 friends in 10 days"

Facebook's early growth team compared cohorts of new users who stayed with those who left. The clearest difference was how quickly people connected with friends: around seven in their first ten days. The team made that their goal and built features like friend suggestions to help new users get there.

Lesson: cohort analysis can turn a vague goal ("retention") into a specific behaviour a team can work on.

Got it wrong

Facebook's video watch time

In 2016, Facebook disclosed that for about two years its "average duration of video viewed" had left out views shorter than three seconds. According to an ad agency's letter reported by the Wall Street Journal, that overstated average viewing time by an estimated 60 to 80%. Advertisers and publishers had made decisions on the inflated number.

Lesson: a metric's definition is part of the product. Write it down, check it, and make sure it measures what its name says.

Key takeaways

  1. Agree a tracking plan: consistent event names and written definitions, tested at every release.
  2. Funnels show where people drop off, not why. Split them by segment before concluding.
  3. Cohorts show whether the product keeps people better over time. Look for curves that flatten.
  4. When a number drops: is it real, when did it start, where is it, and is it rate or mix?
