Users and segmentation

Segments that predict what people do, personas that help instead of decorate, finding segments in your data, and choosing who to serve first.

You're the PM for a language-learning app. Marketing proposes three target segments for next quarter: "Gen Z", "Millennials" and "Gen X".

Show a model answer

1. Generations describe people; they rarely predict what people do. A 22-year-old and a 45-year-old moving to Spain next month have more in common, for this app, than two random 22-year-olds.

2. Split by need and situation: "moving abroad soon", "exam or job requirement", "travel next summer", "hobby learner". Then check in the data which group keeps practising after 30 days.

3. Pick the segment with the sharpest pain and a deadline, that you can reach cheaply, and whose members talk to each other. People with an exam date often fit, and you can find them where they prepare.

Lessons

The idea, the framework, a real example, where it breaks, and a quick drill.

1Segments that predict behaviourDescribing people is easy. Predicting them is useful.8 min

The idea

A segment is a group of users you treat differently because they behave differently. If two groups would get the same product, message and price, splitting them is just a label.

The framework

DescriptiveAge, country, company size, job title. Easy to get, weak at predicting.
BehaviouralWhat people do: features used, how often, what they buy. Strong at predicting.
Needs-basedThe job, situation and goal. Strongest for deciding what to build.

A good segment passes four tests: different behaviour or needs, big enough to matter, reachable through some channel, and actionable: you'd change something for them.

Real example

A project tool sees 6% monthly churn. Split by company size, churn barely changes. Split by "invited a teammate in their first week", it's roughly 2% versus 10%. Only the second split tells the team what to do: get people to invite someone early.

Where it breaks

  • Too many segments. If you can't name a different action for each one, merge them.
  • Behaviour can be a result, not a cause. Power users use more features because they're engaged; forcing features on others won't make them engaged. Test before you act.

Drill: what kind of segment is this?

"Users who exported a report in their first week"

"Freelancers who need to send invoices from their phone between client visits"

"Companies with 50 to 200 employees in Germany"

2Personas and their limitsA persona should change a decision, or it's decoration.7 min

The idea

A persona turns a segment into a person the team can picture. Done well, it keeps real users in the room. Done badly, it's a stock photo, a made-up name and a hobby list that nobody uses.

The framework

  1. Start from research, not imagination. A persona invented in a workshop is a hypothesis; label it as one.
  2. Lead with the job and situation (M2), then behaviours, then frustrations. Put demographics last, or leave them out.
  3. Add what would change their mind: what they use today, and what makes them switch.
  4. Test it: does it help decide between two options? If not, cut it.

Real example

Weak: "Marketing Mary, 34, likes yoga and lattes." Strong: "Agency account manager who reports to five clients every Monday. Builds reports by hand from three tools, which takes four hours. Would switch if reports were client-ready without editing." The second one tells you what to build first.

Where it breaks

  • Personas freeze. Users change; a persona from two years ago may now be wrong.
  • Stereotypes creep in. Irrelevant details like age, gender or hobbies invite bias without adding insight.

Drill

Which persona detail is most useful for product decisions?

3Finding segments in your dataLet behaviour show you who your best users are.9 min

The idea

Your users have already sorted themselves by what they do. Find the group that gets the most value, understand what they have in common, then find and build for more people like them.

The framework

  1. Cut by behaviour: how recently and how often people use the product, and what they spend or do. Compare retention for each group.
  2. Ask the people who love it. Rahul Vohra at Superhuman used Sean Ellis's question: "How would you feel if you could no longer use this product?" Ellis suggests that if around 40% or more say "very disappointed", you're near product-market fit.
  3. Profile that group: what jobs, roles and situations do they share? That's your sharpest segment.

Real example

As Vohra described in First Round Review, Superhuman's first survey came in well below 40% "very disappointed". Instead of averaging everyone, the team looked at who answered "very disappointed", described them precisely, and focused the roadmap on what those users loved and what held back the "somewhat disappointed" users who looked like them. The score climbed over the following quarters.

Where it breaks

  • Small numbers lie. With a few dozen answers, a segment can look strong by chance.
  • Survivors only. Surveying current users ignores everyone who already left. Interview churned users too.

Drill

22% of surveyed users say they'd be "very disappointed" without your product. Best next step?

4Choosing a beachheadWin one segment completely before you try to serve everyone.8 min

The idea

Geoffrey Moore's Crossing the Chasm borrows a military metaphor: land on one beach, win it completely, then expand. A small segment that loves you and recommends you beats a big one that's lukewarm.

The framework

Score each candidate segment on:

  1. Pain: how badly do they need this, and how soon?
  2. Reach: can you find them cheaply?
  3. Willingness to pay or engage.
  4. References: do they talk to each other, so one win leads to the next?
  5. Next beach: does winning here make the next segment easier?

Real example

Facebook launched in 2004 for Harvard students only, then expanded school by school before opening to everyone in 2006. Each campus was dense with people who knew each other, so the product was useful immediately and spread by word of mouth.

Where it breaks

  • A beach too small to sustain the business, with no obvious next one.
  • Staying too long. The beachhead is a start, not a strategy. Plan the next segment early.

Drill

Which beachhead is strongest for a new tool that helps clinics manage appointment no-shows?

Cases

One team that narrowed down to the users who loved it, one that bet big on a segment it never checked.

Got it right

Superhuman

Superhuman, a premium email app, measured how many users would be "very disappointed" without it. Rather than build for everyone, the team defined the users who loved it most, then prioritised features that served them and the users most like them. Its score rose substantially over the next year.

Lesson: a sharp segment beats an average of everyone.

Got it wrong

Quibi

Quibi raised about $1.75 billion to make premium, short episodes for people watching on their phones on the go. It launched in April 2020, as lockdowns kept people at home, and competed with free short video on TikTok and YouTube. It shut down about six months later.

Lesson: the target segment, "busy people with spare minutes on the move", was assumed, not proven, and it vanished overnight.

Show a worked example

Product: a language-learning app.

Segment: people moving abroad within six months. A real deadline, findable in relocation and expat groups, and they recommend what worked to each other.

Persona: a software engineer who has accepted a job in Madrid, starting in four months. Practises on the train for 15 minutes a day. Has tried two apps and quit both because the lessons felt like games, not like the conversations she'll actually have. Would switch to anything that gets her through a flat viewing and a doctor's appointment.

Not yet: hobby learners and students. They need different motivation and are less likely to pay. Serve them once the first segment loves the product.

Key takeaways

  1. Segment by what people do and need, not only by who they are.
  2. A segment is real only if you'd treat it differently.
  3. Personas are research, not fiction. Lead with the job, situation and frustration.
  4. Win one dense, reachable beachhead first, and plan the next beach early.
