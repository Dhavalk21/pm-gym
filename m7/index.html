Lessons

Each one runs the same way: the idea, the framework, a real example, where it breaks, and a quick drill.

1User flows and hierarchyEvery screen should make the next step obvious.8 min

The idea

A user flow is the path someone takes to get a job done: every screen, choice and wait in between. Visual hierarchy is how a screen tells people what matters most. Most usability problems are a flow with an unclear next step.

The framework

  1. Start from the job (M2): what is the person trying to finish?
  2. Map the happy path, then the edge cases: errors, empty states, going back.
  3. One primary action per screen. Make it the most visible thing.
  4. Remove steps that don't help the user decide or act.

Real example

A food app's checkout has five screens: basket, address, delivery time, payment, review. Watching users shows people re-check the address on the review screen anyway. Merging address and time into one screen, with the address pre-filled, cuts a step and the doubt that came with it.

Where it breaks

  • Fewer steps isn't always better. A clear three-step flow beats a crowded single page. Optimise for clarity, not click count.
  • Flows drift. Every new feature adds a branch. Re-walk your key flows every quarter.

Drill: what's the problem?

A settings page has eight buttons of the same size and colour. People can't find "Save". What's wrong?

2Usability heuristicsTen rules of thumb that catch most problems fast.9 min

The idea

In 1994, Jakob Nielsen published ten usability heuristics: broad rules that good interfaces follow. A heuristic review, walking a screen against them, finds many problems in an hour, before any user testing.

The framework

Show what's happeningProgress, status, confirmation.
Speak the user's languageTheir words, formats and order.
Let people undo and leaveExits, undo, cancel.
Prevent errors, then explain themConstraints first; clear, fixable messages second.

The others: keep things consistent, show options instead of making people remember them, offer shortcuts for experts, cut clutter, and provide help where it's needed.

Real example

A banking app shows "Error 0x83" when a transfer fails. Two heuristics fail at once: it doesn't speak the user's language, and it doesn't help them recover. "Your daily limit is €1,000. Send €600 today and the rest tomorrow, or raise your limit" fixes both.

Where it breaks

  • Heuristics find problems, not priorities. A review can list 40 issues; users decide which five matter.
  • Experts miss things real users hit. Use reviews to clean up before testing, not instead of it.

Drill: which heuristic?

A long form loses everything you typed if you press the browser's back button.

3Prototypes and usability testsWatch five people try it before you build it.9 min

The idea

A prototype is a cheap, fake version of a design, from paper sketches to clickable mock-ups. A usability test gives real users realistic tasks with that prototype and watches where they struggle.

The framework

  1. Match fidelity to the question. Sketches for the flow, clickable mock-ups for the details.
  2. Give tasks, not questions: "Book a table for four on Friday," not "Do you like this?"
  3. Ask people to think aloud, and stay quiet when they struggle.
  4. Test with about five people per round and per user type, then fix and test again.

Real example

Nielsen Norman Group's research found that about five users uncover most of a design's usability problems in one round of testing. Several small rounds, fixing in between, find more than one big study.

Where it breaks

  • Five users is for finding problems, not proving numbers. To measure a rate, like completion, you need far more people, or an A/B test (M11).
  • Prototypes can't test everything. Performance, real data and long-term habits only show up in the real product.

Drill: which approach?

You want to know if people can complete a new checkout flow. What's best?

4AccessibilityDesign for everyone, including people with disabilities. It's also the law.8 min

The idea

Accessible products work for people who see, hear, move or think differently: screen-reader users, people who can't use a mouse, people with low vision or colour blindness. It helps everyone, like captions in a noisy train, and in the EU it's increasingly a legal requirement.

The framework

  1. Contrast: body text at least 4.5:1 against its background.
  2. Keyboard: everything works without a mouse, with a visible focus.
  3. Labels and alt text: every input labelled, every meaningful image described.
  4. Never colour alone: pair colour with text or icons.

These come from the Web Content Accessibility Guidelines (WCAG), published by the W3C.

Real example

The European Accessibility Act has applied since 28 June 2025 to many consumer digital products and services in the EU, including e-commerce, banking and e-books. A shop whose checkout can't be completed with a keyboard or screen reader now has a compliance problem, not just a design one.

Where it breaks

  • Checklists aren't the whole story. A page can pass automated checks and still be hard to use. Test with disabled users where you can.
  • Retrofitting is expensive. Build it in from the first design, not after launch.

Drill: spot the issue

Form errors are shown only by turning the field border red.

Cases

One team that made a whole government simpler, one redesign that users rejected loudly.

Got it right

GOV.UK

In 2012, the UK's Government Digital Service replaced hundreds of separate government websites with one: GOV.UK. Its design principles started with user needs and "do the hard work to make it simple": plain language, one clear path per task, and accessibility from the start. The site won the Design Museum's Design of the Year award in 2013.

Lesson: simplicity is work. Clear flows and plain words beat clever features.

Got it wrong

Snapchat's 2018 redesign

In early 2018, Snapchat reorganised its app, mixing friends' stories with publishers' content in new places. Many users found it confusing. Over a million signed a petition asking for the old design back, and Snap reversed parts of the change later that year.

Lesson: changing a flow millions of people use by habit needs testing with those people first, and a gradual rollout.

Show a worked example

Product: a restaurant booking app. Job: book a table for four on Friday evening. Six steps today.

Hardest step: choosing a time. Full slots look exactly like free ones until you tap them, so people tap three or four times and give up. That breaks "show what's happening" and "prevent errors".

Test: a clickable prototype that greys out full slots and suggests the nearest free time, tried by five regular guests with the task "book for four this Friday".

Key takeaways

  1. Every screen needs one obvious next step. Design flows from the job, not the feature list.
  2. Usability heuristics catch many problems in an hour. Run a review before you test.
  3. Watch five people try a prototype before you build. Give tasks, not questions.
  4. Accessibility is part of the design, and in the EU, part of the law.
