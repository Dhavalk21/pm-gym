Market and competition

Sizing a market from buyers you can count, seeing who you really compete with, positioning so the right people get it at once, and asking why now.

A founder pitches a booking app for independent hair salons in Germany: "The beauty market is worth billions, and we have no competitors."

Skip to lessons
Show a model answer

1. Bottom-up: count independent salons you could reach, multiply by a realistic monthly price, then estimate the share you could win in a few years. The beauty market's total says nothing about what salons pay for software.

2. "No competitors" is never true. Salons already book by phone, paper diaries, WhatsApp, Instagram messages, and general booking platforms. Those habits are the competition, and they're free.

3. A good "why now": customers increasingly expect to book online at any hour, and salons struggle with no-shows they could cut with reminders and deposits. A weak "why now": "apps are popular".

Lessons

The idea, the framework, a real example, where it breaks, and a quick drill.

1Sizing a market, bottom-upStart from buyers you can count, not "1% of billions".9 min

The idea

Market size answers one question: is this worth doing, and how big could it get? Top-down estimates start from a big industry number and take a slice. Bottom-up estimates multiply buyers you can count by what they'd realistically pay. Investors and leaders trust the second, because you can check every input.

The framework

TAMEveryone who could ever buy
SAMThe part you can serve and reach
SOMWhat you can realistically win, soon

Bottom-up: number of target buyers × price per year = TAM. Narrow by who you can actually serve and reach = SAM. Apply a realistic share over 2 to 3 years = SOM.

Real example

Illustrative numbers for a shift-planning tool: 60,000 independent restaurants in target countries × 3 managers × €40 a month = about €86 million a year (TAM). Only those in countries you can sell in, with 10 or more staff, maybe a third of that (SAM). Winning a few percent of SAM in three years is the honest SOM.

Where it breaks

  • New categories have no data. When a market doesn't exist yet, size the job (M2): how many people have it, and what do they spend on it today?
  • Precision theatre. A sizing is a range with stated assumptions, not a number to two decimals.

2Who you really compete withYour biggest competitor is often a spreadsheet, or doing nothing.8 min

The idea

Customers compare you with whatever they'd do if you didn't exist. That's usually not the product most like yours. It's the job from M2: anything else hired for it.

The framework

DirectProducts like yours, solving the same job the same way.
IndirectDifferent products for the same job: a general tool, an agency, a freelancer.
SubstitutesSpreadsheets, email, paper, WhatsApp. Cheap, familiar, good enough.
Doing nothingLiving with the problem. Often the hardest to beat.

Then map them on the two dimensions customers care about most, which you learn in interviews (M5), not the ones that flatter you.

Real example

For a team task tool, the obvious rivals are other task tools. But in interviews, most small teams say they use a shared spreadsheet and a chat group. To win, you must be clearly better than "free and already open in another tab", which is a very different bar.

Where it breaks

  • Feature grids. Ticking boxes against rivals says nothing about why customers switch.
  • Obsessing over rivals pulls the roadmap towards copying them (M1's competitor trap) instead of serving customers.

3PositioningMake the right customers get it at once.9 min

The idea

Positioning is the context you set so that the right customers understand why your product matters to them. April Dunford's book Obviously Awesome breaks it into five parts that build on each other. Read more from her.

The framework

  1. Competitive alternatives: what customers would use if you didn't exist.
  2. Unique attributes: what you have that those alternatives don't.
  3. Value: what those attributes do for customers, with proof.
  4. Best-fit customers: who cares most about that value.
  5. Market category: the frame that makes your value obvious to them.

Real example

A shift-planning tool. Alternatives: a spreadsheet and a WhatsApp group. Unique: it knows each person's availability and labour rules. Value: rotas in 15 minutes instead of 3 hours, with no rule breaches. Best fit: independent restaurants with 10 to 40 staff. Category: "rota planning for restaurants", not "workforce management platform", which signals enterprise prices and complexity.

Where it breaks

  • Positioning for everyone lands with no one. Pick the best-fit customer and accept losing the rest for now (M3).
  • Inventing a new category is expensive. You must teach the market what it is before you can sell to it.

4Why now?Good ideas at the wrong time still fail.6 min

The idea

Something has to have changed for an opportunity to be open now and not five years ago. Without a clear "why now", the market is probably either taken or not ready.

The framework

Look for a recent shift in one of these:

  1. Technology: something became possible or cheap. Language models made understanding messy text affordable.
  2. Regulation: a rule creates or removes demand.
  3. Behaviour: people started or stopped doing something at scale.
  4. Economics: costs, prices or budgets moved.

Real example

Regulation as a "why now": since January 2025, businesses in Germany must be able to receive electronic invoices from other businesses, with sending becoming mandatory in phases over the following years. Every small business needs a way to handle e-invoices, which opens a market for simple tools that didn't need to exist before.

Where it breaks

  • Too early is the same as wrong. A real shift can still be years away from mainstream behaviour.
  • Hype isn't a shift. "Everyone is talking about it" doesn't mean customers' behaviour has changed.

Cases

One newcomer that won a crowded market with sharp positioning, one giant that entered without a reason for anyone to switch.

Got it right

Zoom

Zoom launched in 2013 into a market full of established video tools. Rather than more features, it positioned around one thing people struggled with: calls that just work, joined with a single link, even on weak connections. That reliability won over businesses well before 2020, when the pandemic made video calls part of daily life.

Lesson: in a crowded market, win on the attribute customers care about and incumbents neglect. And when a big "why now" arrived, Zoom was ready for it.

Got it wrong

Google+

Google launched Google+ in 2011 to compete with Facebook. It had real strengths, like "Circles" for sharing with different groups. But people's friends were already on Facebook, so there was little reason to move. Usage stayed thin, and Google shut down the consumer version in 2019.

Lesson: the competitive alternative was "stay where my friends are". A better feature set couldn't beat that.

Show a worked example

Product: an e-invoicing app for German tradespeople, like electricians and plumbers. All numbers illustrative.

Size: say 400,000 small trade businesses × €15 a month = about €72 million a year (TAM). Those not already covered by accounting software, maybe 40% (SAM, about €29 million). 3% of SAM in three years is about €0.9 million a year (SOM).

Alternatives: their tax adviser's software, the invoicing module in bank apps, Word templates, and waiting until the deadline forces them.

Why now: since January 2025 they must be able to receive e-invoices, and issuing them becomes mandatory in the coming years.

Key takeaways

  1. Size bottom-up: buyers you can count × a real price, then what you can reach and win.
  2. Your real competitors include spreadsheets, habits and doing nothing.
  3. Position against the alternatives, for the customers who care most.
  4. Name the shift that makes now the right time. If there isn't one, be suspicious.
