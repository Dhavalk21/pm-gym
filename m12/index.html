Zone 4, Grow

Growth

Getting new users to real value fast, keeping them, building loops that compound, and letting the product do the selling.

Lessons

Each one runs the same way: the idea, the framework, a real example, where it breaks, and a quick drill.

1Activation: the first moment of valueGet new users to the point where they get it, fast.8 min

The idea

Activation is the moment a new user first gets real value, often called the aha moment. People who reach it are far more likely to stay. Good growth teams find the specific action that predicts retention, then remove everything that slows new users down on the way to it.

The framework

  1. Find the activation action: compare users who stayed with those who left (M10). What did the stayers do early on?
  2. Measure time to value: how long from sign-up to that action?
  3. Remove steps that don't help: fewer fields, defaults, templates, sample data.
  4. Guide, don't lecture: one next step at a time, inside the product, not a long tour.

Real example

Slack's team reported that once a team had exchanged about 2,000 messages, around 93% of those teams were still using Slack. That's when a team has really tried it. Onboarding focused on getting teams there: inviting teammates, creating channels and connecting tools they already used.

Where it breaks

  • The action may be a sign of value, not its cause. Test whether pushing people towards it actually improves retention (M11).
  • Activation targets get gamed too (M9): 2,000 automated messages wouldn't mean a team gets value.

Drill: find the aha

For a team task-management app, which is the best activation action to aim for?

2Retention before acquisitionFilling a leaky bucket is the most expensive kind of growth.9 min

The idea

If people don't stay, every new user you pay for is a temporary visitor. Retention is the base of all growth: it makes every acquisition euro go further, feeds word of mouth, and makes loops possible. That's why experienced growth teams fix the leaky bucket before turning up the tap.

The framework

  1. Measure retention at the product's natural frequency: daily for messaging, weekly for groceries, yearly for tax software.
  2. Look for the curve to flatten (M10). A flat line means a group of people who keep getting value.
  3. Improve it in three places: first week (activation), ongoing value (habits, new uses), and bringing back people who left (resurrection).

Real example

Two apps each win 10,000 new users a month. App A keeps 20% after three months; App B keeps 40%. After a year, App B has roughly twice the active users from the same marketing spend, and its users bring friends. Doubling A's ad budget would cost far more than fixing what makes people leave.

Where it breaks

  • Some products are used rarely by design. Judge a travel or tax app by yearly return, not daily use.
  • Retention can be bought badly: hard-to-cancel subscriptions keep people paying, not valuing (M4). That's not retention, it's sludge.

Drill: where to invest?

Your app keeps only 5% of new users after a month. Marketing wants to double ad spend. What do you do first?

3Growth loopsFunnels end. Loops feed themselves.9 min

The idea

A funnel takes people from step to step and stops. A growth loop turns the output of one group of users into the input for the next: users invite users, users create content that search engines find, or customers' profit pays for more ads. Loops compound; funnels don't.

The framework

  1. Viral loop: using the product exposes others to it (shared links, invites, documents).
  2. Content loop: users create content that brings new visitors (reviews, answers, templates).
  3. Paid loop: each customer earns back more than they cost to win, funding more ads.
  4. Measure loop strength: new users each user brings (invites × conversion), or euros returned per euro spent.

Real example

Dropbox struggled to make paid search ads profitable. Its referral programme gave extra free storage to both the person inviting and the friend who joined. In April 2010 alone, users sent about 2.8 million referral invites, and Dropbox's CEO Drew Houston said the programme permanently increased sign-ups by 60%.

Where it breaks

  • A loop strength above 1 is rare and doesn't last. Most loops amplify other channels rather than replacing them.
  • Loops need a product worth sharing. Referral rewards can't create word of mouth that doesn't exist.

Drill: loop strength?

Each user invites 5 people, and 10% of invited people sign up. What's the loop strength?

4Product-led growthLet the product do the selling.8 min

The idea

In product-led growth, people try the product themselves (free, freemium or a trial) and upgrade when they hit real value, often without talking to sales. It works when a product is quick to start, shows value fast, and spreads from one user to their team.

The framework

  1. Choose the model: a free tier forever (freemium), a time-limited trial, or both.
  2. Put limits where value is proven: the free plan should be useful, and the paid one should be obviously better once people rely on it.
  3. Spot ready-to-buy accounts from usage, not forms: teams that grow fast or hit limits are product-qualified leads.
  4. Add sales where it helps: big companies still need security reviews and contracts.

Real example

Slack's free plan let teams use the core product fully, but limited how much message history they could search. A team that relied on Slack soon wanted older messages, and the people asking to upgrade had already proved the value to themselves.

Where it breaks

  • Free users cost money in servers and support. Watch the cost of the free tier against the upgrades it produces.
  • Not every product fits. Complex, regulated or top-down purchases, like hospital systems, usually need sales.

Drill: fits PLG?

Which product suits product-led growth best?

Cases

One company that turned its users into its best growth channel, and one that spent huge sums on acquisition people didn't stay for.

Got it right

Dropbox's referral programme

Paid ads were too expensive for Dropbox's price. So it built referrals into the product: invite a friend and both of you get extra free storage, the thing users wanted most. In April 2010 alone, users sent about 2.8 million referral invites, and CEO Drew Houston said the programme permanently increased sign-ups by 60%.

Lesson: the best loops reward people with more of the product's own value, and only work because the product was already worth recommending.

Got it wrong

Quibi

Quibi raised $1.75 billion and spent heavily on star-studded short shows for phones, launched in April 2020 with 90-day free trials. Sensor Tower estimated only about 8% of early trial users became paying subscribers. Quibi shut down six months after launch.

Lesson: money can buy sign-ups, not retention. Without a reason to stay, acquisition just fills a leaky bucket faster.

Key takeaways

  1. Find the activation moment that predicts retention, and shorten the path to it.
  2. Retention comes before acquisition. Fix the leaky bucket before you pour in more.
  3. Loops compound; funnels don't. Measure loop strength and improve its weakest step.
  4. Product-led growth lets people try first and upgrade once they rely on it.
