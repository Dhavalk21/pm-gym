Zone 3, Measure

Evals and AI economics

How to know an AI feature is good enough to ship, how to grade AI answers, how to design for hallucinations, and how to keep AI costs in line with what users pay.

1Why AI features need evalsA good demo proves nothing. A test set does.8 min

The idea

Ordinary code gives the same output for the same input. A language model doesn't: the same question can get a different answer, and a small prompt change can fix one case and break five others. An eval is how you know whether an AI feature is good: a set of test cases, criteria for what a good answer looks like, and a score you can compare over time.

The framework

  1. Collect real cases: a few hundred actual user questions or tasks, including hard and awkward ones.
  2. Write down what "good" means: correct, based on our sources, complete, safe, right tone.
  3. Score every version of the prompt, sources or model on the same set.
  4. Set a launch bar in advance, and re-run the eval after every change.

Real example

Morgan Stanley tested every AI use case against real-world tasks, graded with help from its own experts, before rolling it out to financial advisors. According to OpenAI's case study, over 98% of advisor teams now actively use its internal AI assistant. Rigorous testing was what made the firm confident enough to ship.

Where it breaks

  • An eval is only as good as its cases. If it misses the questions users really ask, a high score means little. Keep adding real failures to it.
  • Offline scores don't show behaviour. Once it passes, still measure real use and outcomes (M9, M11).

2How to grade AI outputCode checks, human rubrics, and AI judges you check.9 min

The idea

There are three ways to grade an answer, from cheapest to most nuanced. Most teams use all three: code for what can be checked mechanically, an AI judge for scale, and humans to set the standard and check the judge.

The framework

  1. Code checks: right format, includes a link, under 100 words, no banned phrases. Fast, cheap and exact.
  2. Human grading with a rubric: experts score answers on written criteria, like correct, grounded, complete, safe and tone.
  3. Model as judge: another model grades with the same rubric, so you can score thousands of answers. Check its grades against human grades on a sample first.

Real example

A help assistant's rubric might be: correct (matches policy), grounded (every claim is in the retrieved sources), complete (nothing important left out), safe (no actions it isn't allowed to take) and helpful tone. A single failure on "grounded" or "safe" fails the whole answer, whatever the other scores.

Where it breaks

  • AI judges have biases: they tend to prefer longer answers and answers in their own style. Re-check them against humans regularly.
  • Average scores hide disasters. Track the worst failures separately; one invented refund promise can cost more than a hundred good answers earn.

3Hallucinations and guardrailsModels state things confidently that aren't true. Design for it.9 min

The idea

A hallucination is a confident answer that isn't supported by facts: an invented policy, a made-up number, a source that doesn't exist. As AI-1 explained, models generate plausible text, not verified facts. You can't remove hallucinations completely, but you can make them rare, easy to catch and low in impact.

The framework

  1. Ground it: give the model your sources, and tell it to answer only from them.
  2. Let it say "I don't know" and hand over to a person when no source fits.
  3. Limit what it can do: it should only take actions you've allowed, whatever a user types. Watch for prompt injection, text that tries to override its instructions.
  4. Use fixed answers or human review for high-stakes topics like refunds, prices, legal or health.

Real example

In 2022, Air Canada's website chatbot told a grieving customer he could claim a bereavement discount after his flight. The real policy said otherwise. In 2024, a Canadian tribunal ruled the airline was responsible for what its chatbot said and ordered it to pay the difference, rejecting the argument that the chatbot was somehow separate from the company.

Where it breaks

  • Too many guardrails make an assistant useless: refusing everything is a failure too. Measure refusals of reasonable questions.
  • Users over-trust confident answers. Showing sources helps people check.

4AI economicsEvery answer has a price and a wait time.8 min

The idea

Each AI request costs money, roughly in proportion to the tokens (pieces of words) it reads and writes, and takes time to respond (latency). Bigger models are usually better, slower and more expensive. The PM's job is to pick the cheapest, fastest setup that still meets the quality bar.

The framework

  1. Set the quality bar per feature from your eval: autocomplete can be rougher than a reply to an angry customer.
  2. Pick the cheapest model that clears it, within the latency the experience needs.
  3. Cut cost: shorter prompts and contexts, caching repeated answers, routing easy requests to smaller models, usage limits.
  4. Check unit economics: AI cost per user per month against what that user pays.

Real example

Lingo adds an AI tutor. Each message reads about 1,500 tokens and writes about 300. At illustrative prices of €3 per million tokens read and €15 per million written, that's about €0.009 a message. A heavy user sending 30 messages a day costs about €8 a month, against a €12.99 subscription. A daily limit, or a smaller model for easy turns, protects the margin.

Where it breaks

  • Prices fall fast, often several times cheaper per year for the same quality. Re-check your model choice regularly.
  • The cheapest model can be the most expensive if its mistakes create support tickets, refunds or lost trust.

Drill: pick the model

Model A scores 92% on your eval and costs 6 times more than Model B, which scores 88%. The feature needs 85%. Which do you pick?

Cases

One firm that tested every AI use case before launch, and one airline that found out it was responsible for its chatbot's words.

Got it right

Morgan Stanley's AI assistant

Before rolling out AI tools to its financial advisors, Morgan Stanley built evals for each use case, such as summarising research and finding the right document, graded with help from its own experts. Only use cases that passed went into production. According to OpenAI's case study, over 98% of advisor teams now actively use the assistant.

Lesson: evals aren't paperwork. They're what let you launch with confidence, and earn users' trust.

Got it wrong

Air Canada's chatbot

A customer asked Air Canada's chatbot about bereavement fares after his grandmother died. It told him he could claim the discount after travelling, which the airline's actual policy didn't allow. In February 2024, a British Columbia tribunal held Air Canada responsible for its chatbot's statements and ordered it to pay damages and fees of about 812 Canadian dollars.

Lesson: whatever your AI says, your company said. Ground answers in real policy, and test the questions where a wrong answer costs money.

Key takeaways

  1. No eval, no launch: real test cases, written criteria, a score you track.
  2. Grade with code where you can, humans to set the standard, and AI judges you've checked.
  3. Design for hallucinations: ground answers, allow "I don't know", limit actions, protect high-stakes topics.
  4. Pick the cheapest, fastest model that clears the bar, and check cost per user against revenue.
