Roadmaps and OKRs

Why feature-and-date roadmaps fail, how Now, Next, Later shows direction honestly, how to write OKRs that measure outcomes, and how to handle dates without losing trust.

1Why feature-and-date roadmaps failFalse certainty turns guesses into promises.8 min

The idea

The classic roadmap is a timeline of features with dates. It looks reassuring, but it hides two problems. It focuses on outputs, not outcomes (M1), so success means shipping, not solving. And it implies certainty nobody has: dates for work that hasn't been designed become promises that sales, customers and leaders plan around.

The framework

  1. Outputs crowd out outcomes: the team ships the list even when learning says to change course.
  2. Dates harden: a rough guess in January becomes a commitment by March.
  3. Estimates are optimistic: people underestimate how long work takes, even when they know similar work overran. Psychologists call it the planning fallacy.

Real example

The planning fallacy's famous example is the Sydney Opera House. Planned for completion in 1963 at a cost of about 7 million Australian dollars, it opened in 1973 for about 102 million, with a smaller scope than designed. Software roadmaps are rarely that dramatic, but the pattern is the same.

Where it breaks

  • Some dates are real: a legal deadline, a conference, a partner launch. Treat those differently (lesson 4).
  • Stakeholders still need to plan. Removing dates without offering something better just moves the anxiety.

2Now, Next, LaterOutcomes, with honest confidence.9 min

The idea

The Now, Next, Later roadmap, popularised by Janna Bastow of ProdPad, replaces dates with time horizons and confidence. Now is what the team is building. Next is being shaped. Later is the direction you're exploring. Items are written as problems or outcomes, not features.

The framework

  1. Write items as problems or outcomes: "Help new users send their first invoice sooner", not "Onboarding wizard v2".
  2. Confidence falls from left to right: Now is detailed and firm; Later is broad and changeable.
  3. Link each item to the strategy and a goal (M14, M9).
  4. Show different views to different audiences: detail for the team, themes for customers.

Real example

Ledgerly's roadmap. Now: help new users send their first invoice sooner; reduce late payments with reminders. Next: instant payouts by card; payment links for a second bank. Later: tax estimates; Spain and Italy. Everyone can see the direction, and nobody mistakes "Later" for a promise.

Where it breaks

  • "Later" can become a dumping ground. Prune it regularly; a long Later list is a wishlist (M14).
  • Some customers need dates. Give ranges for Now items, and be clear that Next and Later aren't commitments.

3OKRsAn objective that inspires, and a few results that prove it.9 min

The idea

Objectives and key results were developed by Andy Grove at Intel in the 1970s and brought to Google in 1999 by investor John Doerr, who later wrote Measure What Matters. The objective is a qualitative, inspiring direction. The key results are three to five measurable outcomes that show whether you got there.

The framework

  1. Objective: what you want to achieve and why it matters. "Get new freelancers to their first paid invoice faster."
  2. Key results: measurable outcomes with a start and a target: "Median days to first paid invoice: 21 → 12."
  3. Not tasks: "Launch reminders" is a way to get there, not a result.
  4. Ambitious, not tied to bonuses: at Google, hitting about 70% of a stretch goal is considered good.

Real example

Compare two key results for the same objective. "Ship the onboarding redesign" is done the day it launches, whether or not anyone benefits. "Share of new accounts sending an invoice in week one: 35% → 50%" is done only when customers actually behave differently. The second leaves the team free to find the best way there.

Where it breaks

  • OKR theatre: a quarterly ritual of writing goals nobody looks at again. Review progress weekly or fortnightly.
  • Sandbagging: if OKRs affect pay or reviews, people set easy targets. Keep them separate.

4Dates, uncertainty and trustFix the date and flex the scope, or the other way round.8 min

The idea

Some dates matter: a legal deadline, a trade fair, a partner's launch. For those, don't pretend you can fix everything. The classic trade-off is between scope, time and resources; adding people late rarely helps. So when the date is fixed, flex the scope, and when the scope is fixed, give a range.

The framework

  1. Fixed date: agree the smallest valuable version, ship that on time, and add more after.
  2. Fixed scope: give a range with a confidence level: "May to June, about 70% confident", and narrow it as you learn.
  3. Update early and openly when things change. Late surprises destroy more trust than honest uncertainty.
  4. Release in small steps so problems show up early, not on launch day.

Real example

Ledgerly must support Germany's new e-invoicing format before the legal deadline. The date can't move, so the team ships the required format first, with a basic screen, months early, then improves the experience in later releases. Customers are never at risk, and the team learns from real use before the deadline.

Where it breaks

  • Ranges can feel evasive to people used to single dates. Explain what makes the range wider or narrower.
  • Flexing scope needs a clear minimum. Agree in advance what "the smallest valuable version" is.

Cases

One company that adopted a simple goal system early and kept it for decades, and one launch where a fixed date and a fixed scope collided.

Got it right

Google and OKRs

In 1999, investor John Doerr taught Google's young founding team the goal-setting system he'd learned from Andy Grove at Intel: objectives and key results. Google adopted OKRs across the company and has used them ever since, with ambitious targets where hitting about 70% counts as success. The approach spread to many other technology companies.

Lesson: a simple, shared way to state outcomes helps a fast-growing company stay focused, as long as it measures results, not tasks.

Got it wrong

HealthCare.gov's launch

The US government's health insurance marketplace launched on 1 October 2013, a fixed date, with its full scope at once. The site was overwhelmed by errors. According to internal notes later released by Congress, only six people managed to enrol on the first day, despite millions of visits.

Lesson: fixing both the date and the full scope, with little end-to-end testing, sets up failure. Fix one, flex the other, and release in steps.

Key takeaways

  1. Feature-and-date roadmaps turn guesses into promises. Plan outcomes instead.
  2. Now, Next, Later shows direction with honest confidence, written as problems to solve.
  3. OKRs: an inspiring objective and three to five measurable outcomes, not tasks.
  4. When a date is fixed, flex the scope. Give ranges, update early, release in steps.
