1Why experiment, and when not toOpinions are cheap. Randomised tests settle arguments.8 min

The idea

When a metric moves after a launch, you rarely know whether the launch caused it: seasons, campaigns and news all move numbers too. An A/B test (a randomised controlled experiment) shows a random half of users the new version and the other half the old one, at the same time. Because only the version differs, a difference in results is very likely caused by the change.

The framework

  1. Test when you have enough users, the change is easy to switch on and off, and you genuinely don't know if it will help.
  2. Don't test when traffic is too low to ever reach a result, the change is a clear fix (a broken button), it's a big strategic bet you'll make anyway, or testing would be unethical.
  3. Alternatives when you can't test: before-and-after with care, rolling out region by region, or qualitative research (M5).

Real example

Experts are bad at predicting which ideas win. Ron Kohavi, who led experimentation at Microsoft, reported that only about a third of ideas tested there improved the metric they were designed to improve. A third did nothing, and a third made things worse. Without tests, all of them would have shipped.

Where it breaks

  • Tests answer "what", not "why". Pair them with interviews or usability tests to understand the reason.
  • Not every decision needs a test. Testing everything can slow a team down and avoid real judgement calls.

2Designing a testDecide everything before you see a single result.9 min

The idea

A good test is designed before it starts: what you expect, how you'll measure it, how many people you need and how long it runs. Deciding these up front stops you from moving the goalposts once results come in.

The framework

  1. Hypothesis: "We believe [change] will [effect] for [who], because [reason]."
  2. One primary metric that decides the test, plus guardrails that must not get worse (M9).
  3. Minimum detectable effect: the smallest change worth acting on. Smaller effects need more users.
  4. Sample size and duration from a calculator, in full weeks, so weekdays and weekends are both included.

Real example

Your checkout converts 4% of visitors. You only care about lifts of at least 10%, from 4.0% to 4.4%. A standard sample size calculator (95% confidence, 80% power) says you need roughly 39,000 visitors in each group. With 10,000 visitors a day, that's about eight days, so you plan two full weeks.

Where it breaks

  • Low-traffic products may never reach enough users. Test bigger changes, or use other evidence.
  • Users can affect each other, as in marketplaces and social apps, so a simple per-user split can give misleading results. Teams then split by city or by time instead.

3Reading resultsConfidence intervals, peeking, and too-good-to-be-true.9 min

The idea

A result is statistically significant when a difference this big would be unlikely if the change had no real effect. The p-value measures that surprise; the usual bar is 0.05. A confidence interval is more useful: it gives the range of effects that fit the data, like "+2% to +6%".

The framework

  1. Read the interval, not just the p-value. Is all of it above zero, and is the low end still worth having?
  2. Don't peek and stop early. Checking daily and stopping at the first p < 0.05 produces many false wins.
  3. Check the split. If a 50/50 test ends with clearly unequal groups (a sample ratio mismatch), something is broken.
  4. Beware fishing. Check 20 metrics and about one will look significant by chance.

Real example

Kohavi calls a related rule Twyman's law: any figure that looks interesting or different is usually wrong. When a Bing test showed an unusually large revenue jump within hours, it triggered a "too good to be true" alert, which usually means a bug. This time the result held up, but only after the team checked.

Where it breaks

  • Significant isn't the same as important. With millions of users, a 0.1% lift can be significant and still not worth the complexity.
  • A p-value of 0.04 doesn't mean a 96% chance the new version is better. It only says how surprising the data would be if there were no difference.

4Deciding, and the limits of testsShip, kill, or learn, and watch for short-lived effects.8 min

The idea

The point of a test is a decision. Agree the decision rule before the test starts: "Ship if checkout completion rises by at least 2% and refunds don't rise." Then follow it, including when your favourite idea loses.

The framework

  1. Ship when the primary metric clearly wins and guardrails hold.
  2. Kill when it clearly loses, when it's flat and costs money to keep, or when a guardrail breaks.
  3. Keep running when it hasn't reached its planned size or the effect is still shifting.
  4. Fix the test when the setup is broken: unequal groups, tracking errors, results fished from many segments.

Real example

Novelty effects fool many teams: people click new things because they're new, and the lift fades within weeks. Some companies keep a small long-term holdout, a group that never gets the change, to check that wins still hold months later.

Where it breaks

  • Tests measure short-term behaviour. Trust, brand and long-term habits move slowly and can be harmed by changes that win this week.
  • Ethics come first. Users don't expect to be experimented on in ways that could harm them. Some tests need review, or shouldn't be run.

Drill: make the call

A test raised clicks by 20% in week one, 8% in week two and 3% in week three. What's going on?

Cases

One small idea that a test proved was worth over $100 million, and one test that ignored what users would consent to.

Got it right

Bing's ad headlines

In 2012, a Microsoft employee suggested showing longer ad headlines on Bing. The idea sat in the backlog for months, judged low priority. When an engineer finally ran it as an A/B test, revenue rose 12%, worth over $100 million a year in the US alone, without hurting key user-experience metrics. It was checked and replicated before anyone celebrated.

Lesson: experts can't reliably predict winners. Cheap tests let good ideas prove themselves.

Got it wrong

Facebook's emotion experiment

In 2012, Facebook researchers changed how many positive or negative posts 689,003 users saw in their News Feed, to see whether it changed the emotions in their own posts. When the study was published in 2014, it caused an outcry: users had not knowingly agreed to have their emotions influenced.

Lesson: being able to run a test doesn't mean you should. Experiments that could harm people's wellbeing need consent and ethical review.

Key takeaways

  1. Test when you're unsure and can measure it; just fix the obvious.
  2. Decide hypothesis, metric, guardrails, size and duration before you start.
  3. Read the confidence interval, don't peek, and distrust anything too good to be true.
  4. Agree the decision rule in advance, watch for novelty effects, and put ethics first.
