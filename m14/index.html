Vision and strategy

The difference between vision, strategy and goals, the kernel of a good strategy, how to spot a bad one, and why strategy means choosing what not to do.

1Vision, strategy and goalsWhere we're going, how we'll win, and how we'll know.8 min

The idea

Three words get mixed up all the time. A vision describes the future you want to create for customers, a few years out. A strategy explains how you'll get there given the obstacles in the way. Goals are the measurable markers that show you're on track. You need all three, and they're not interchangeable.

The framework

  1. Vision: the destination, inspiring and customer-centred. "Every freelancer in Europe gets paid on time, without chasing."
  2. Strategy: the route, with the choices that make it possible. "Win EU freelancers by getting them paid faster; no enterprise features."
  3. Goals: the milestones. "Average days to payment from 34 to 20 by year end."

Real example

A good product vision is concrete enough to say no with. "Delight customers" can justify anything. "Every freelancer in Europe gets paid on time, without chasing" tells a team that a new expense-tracking feature probably isn't the priority, while automatic payment reminders probably are.

Where it breaks

  • Visions can become slogans that nobody uses to decide anything. Test it: has it changed a decision lately?
  • Strategies need updating when the diagnosis changes. A strategy that never changes probably isn't being used.

2The kernel of good strategyDiagnosis, guiding policy, coherent actions.9 min

The idea

In Good Strategy Bad Strategy, Richard Rumelt argues that every good strategy has the same core, which he calls the kernel. It's short, specific and uncomfortable, because it makes choices.

The framework

  1. Diagnosis: what's really going on? Name the critical challenge, simply.
  2. Guiding policy: the overall approach to overcoming it, which rules some options out.
  3. Coherent actions: a few concrete steps that reinforce each other and carry out the policy.

Real example

Rumelt's favourite example is Steve Jobs's return to Apple in 1997. The diagnosis: Apple was close to running out of money, with a confusing range of products. The guiding policy: radical focus. The actions: cut the product line down to four computers, consumer and professional, desktop and laptop, and cut much of the rest. Apple returned to profit the next year.

Where it breaks

  • A kernel isn't a plan for everything. It focuses effort; teams still need roadmaps and goals (M16).
  • The diagnosis can be wrong. Write down the assumptions behind it, and revisit them.

3Spotting bad strategyFluff, goals in disguise, and dodging the problem.8 min

The idea

Rumelt also lists the hallmarks of bad strategy. They're everywhere, often on the slides of very capable people, because they feel safer than real choices.

The framework

  1. Fluff: impressive words with no substance. "Synergistic customer-centric innovation."
  2. Goals mistaken for strategy: "Grow 40%" is a target, not a plan.
  3. Failure to face the challenge: actions that ignore the actual problem.
  4. Wishlists: long lists of priorities that don't connect or that contradict each other.

Real example

A meditation app knows people leave after the free trial. Its "strategy" is to launch in five countries, build a smartwatch app and refresh the brand. Every item might be fine on its own, but none deals with the real problem. More countries just bring more people who'll leave after the trial.

Where it breaks

  • Calling something bad strategy can feel political. Ask questions instead: "What's the main obstacle? Which of these addresses it?"
  • Ambitious goals aren't bad. They just need a strategy underneath them.

"Our strategy: be the cheapest, the fastest and the most premium." What's wrong?

4Strategy is choosing what not to doTrade-offs, focus and advantages that last.9 min

The idea

Michael Porter's famous line: the essence of strategy is choosing what not to do. Every real strategy has trade-offs: serving one group well often means serving another less well. A strategy that doesn't make anyone uncomfortable probably isn't one.

The framework

  1. Choose where to play: which customers, needs and markets, and which not.
  2. Choose how to win there: lower cost, a better experience, or a unique offer.
  3. Build activities that fit together, so competitors can't copy one piece.
  4. Look for lasting advantages: network effects, switching costs, scale, brand or unique data.

Real example

Porter's classic example is Southwest Airlines. It chose short, cheap, frequent flights, and said no to meals, assigned seats, connecting baggage and mixed aircraft. Each "no" supported quick turnarounds at the gate, which kept planes flying more hours and fares low. Rivals could copy one piece, but not the whole system.

Where it breaks

  • Markets change. A choice that made sense five years ago can become a trap. Revisit trade-offs when the diagnosis shifts.
  • "No" needs explaining. Teams and customers accept trade-offs better when they understand the strategy behind them.

Cases

One company that turned around by cutting almost everything, and one that invented the future but couldn't face it.

Got it right

Apple in 1997

When Steve Jobs returned, Apple was losing money with a sprawling, confusing range of products. He reduced the computer line to four products in a simple grid, consumer and professional, desktop and laptop, and dropped much of the rest. The focus freed money and attention for fewer, better products, and Apple returned to profit in 1998.

Lesson: a clear diagnosis and the courage to cut can matter more than any new idea.

Got it wrong

Kodak and the digital camera

A Kodak engineer, Steve Sasson, built one of the first digital cameras in 1975. But film was enormously profitable, and Kodak's strategy kept protecting it while digital photography grew. By the time it fully committed, others led the market. Kodak filed for bankruptcy protection in 2012.

Lesson: a strategy that avoids facing the real challenge, here the decline of film, fails however strong the company is.

Key takeaways

  1. Vision is the destination, strategy is the route, goals are the milestones.
  2. A good strategy has a kernel: diagnosis, guiding policy, coherent actions.
  3. Watch for fluff, goals in disguise, wishlists and actions that dodge the real problem.
  4. Strategy means trade-offs: if it doesn't say what you won't do, it's probably a wishlist.
