Zone 5, Decide

Prioritisation

Using strategy as the first filter, scoring frameworks and their limits, cost of delay, and how to say no without losing trust.

The situation

The backlog has 140 items. Sales wants a feature for a big prospect, support wants bug fixes, the CEO wants AI features, and 300 users voted for dark mode. Your lead asks:

"So what goes into next quarter?"

Why predict first? Trying a question before the lesson makes the lesson stick better, even when the first guess is wrong. Rating your confidence trains you to notice what you don't know yet.

Lessons

Each one runs the same way: the idea, the framework, a real example, where it breaks, and a quick drill.

1Why prioritising is hardEverything looks important. Strategy is the first filter.8 min

The idea

Every yes is a no to something else: that's opportunity cost. The backlog will always hold more good ideas than a team can build, plus requests from sales, support, leadership and users, each sure theirs matters most. Prioritising means choosing the work that best serves the strategy, and accepting that good ideas wait.

The framework

  1. Filter by strategy first (M14): does it serve our chosen customers and goal? If not, it waits, however loud it is.
  2. Turn requests into problems (M2): "approval workflows" might really be "I need my manager to sign off", which may have a cheaper fix.
  3. Compare outcomes, not features: which item moves the metric that matters (M9)?
  4. Make the trade-off visible: "If we do this, that moves out."

Real example

A common trap: the loudest request wins. A big prospect's feature, the CEO's idea, or the most-voted item jumps the queue. None of these are wrong signals, but none are the strategy either. A visible strategy filter turns a political fight into a shared question: does this help our chosen customers get our chosen outcome?

Where it breaks

  • Strategy doesn't decide everything. Legal deadlines, security issues and broken basics may need doing regardless.
  • Prioritising isn't a one-off. Revisit as you learn; a plan that never changes isn't using new information.

2Scoring frameworksUseful for discussion. Dangerous as calculators.9 min

The idea

Scoring frameworks make assumptions visible so a team can argue about the right things. The most common is RICE, popularised by Intercom: Reach × Impact × Confidence ÷ Effort. Others include ICE (impact, confidence, ease), MoSCoW (must, should, could, won't) and a simple value-versus-effort grid.

The framework

  1. Reach: how many people it affects in a period.
  2. Impact: how much it helps each one, on a simple scale (Intercom uses 0.25 to 3).
  3. Confidence: how sure you are, as a percentage. This keeps exciting guesses honest.
  4. Effort: person-weeks or months of work.

Real example

Payment reminders reach 2,000 customers a quarter, with high impact (2) and 80% confidence from a prototype test, for 4 person-weeks: 2,000 × 2 × 0.8 ÷ 4 = 800. Dark mode reaches 3,000 people but with low impact (0.25), 100% confidence and 2 weeks: 375. The score starts the conversation; it doesn't end it.

Where it breaks

  • False precision: RICE scores look exact but rest on guesses. A 410 versus a 390 means nothing.
  • Easy to game: anyone who wants their feature can nudge impact up. Agree scales together and review scores as a team.

3Cost of delayWhat does waiting cost? Do the quick, valuable things first.9 min

The idea

Cost of delay, a term popularised by product-development author Don Reinertsen, asks: how much value do we lose for each week this isn't done? Some items lose value fast (a legal deadline, a seasonal feature); others can wait with little loss. Combining it with duration gives a simple ordering rule.

The framework

  1. Estimate weekly cost of delay: revenue, savings or risk lost for each week it isn't live.
  2. Divide by how long it takes (sometimes called CD3).
  3. Do the highest ratio first: short, valuable work ahead of long work of similar value.
  4. Watch deadlines: a fixed date can make cost of delay jump from zero to huge.

Real example

Feature A is worth €10,000 a week and takes 4 weeks. Feature B is worth €4,000 a week and takes 1 week. A first: A is live after 4 weeks, B after 5, losing 4 × €10,000 + 5 × €4,000 = €60,000. B first: B after 1 week, A after 5, losing €4,000 + €50,000 = €54,000. Doing the quick one first saves €6,000.

Where it breaks

  • Weekly value is hard to estimate. Rough ranges are fine; the point is to compare.
  • Not all value is money. Learning, risk reduction and trust matter too. Estimate them honestly rather than ignoring them.

Drill: which first?

4Saying noThe most important word in product, said kindly.8 min

The idea

Every roadmap is a series of noes. Saying no well keeps trust with the people you disappoint: they understand why, they feel heard, and they know what would change the answer. Saying it badly, or not at all, creates hidden backlogs, broken promises and frustrated stakeholders.

The framework

  1. Acknowledge the request and the problem behind it.
  2. Explain the trade-off in terms of the strategy: "This would delay X for 12,000 customers."
  3. Offer an alternative where you can: a workaround, a partner, a lighter version.
  4. Say what would change your mind: "If three more customers need it, we'll revisit."

Real example

When Steve Jobs returned to Apple in 1997, he told developers that focus means saying no to good ideas, not just bad ones. For a PM, that's daily work: the request is usually reasonable, the person asking is usually right about their problem, and the answer is still "not now".

Where it breaks

  • Don't say no to everything. Patterns in requests can reveal a real problem worth solving (M2).
  • "Not now" must sometimes become "now". Keep a visible list and actually revisit it, or people stop believing you.

Drill: best reply?

Sales asks for a feature that's off-strategy. Which reply is best?

Cases

One team that cut almost everything to find its product, and one company that rebuilt everything and lost its users.

Got it right

Burbn becomes Instagram

Burbn was an app with check-ins, plans, points and photo sharing. Its founders, Kevin Systrom and Mike Krieger, noticed that people mostly used the photo features. They cut nearly everything else and relaunched as Instagram in October 2010. About 25,000 people signed up on the first day, and it passed one million users within about two months.

Lesson: prioritising sometimes means removing, not adding. Watch what people actually use.

Got it wrong

Digg v4

In August 2010, the popular news site Digg launched v4, a big rebuild that removed features its core users loved and favoured publishers. Launch was buggy, users protested, and many moved to Reddit. Digg, which had raised money at a valuation of about $164 million in 2008, was sold in 2012 for a reported $500,000.

Lesson: prioritising a big rewrite over the core users' needs, and launching it all at once, can destroy what made a product valuable.

Key takeaways

  1. Strategy is the first filter. Turn requests into problems before you compare them.
  2. Use RICE and similar scores to structure the debate, not to end it.
  3. Cost of delay: short, valuable work first, and respect real deadlines.
  4. Say no kindly and clearly: acknowledge, explain the trade-off, offer an alternative, say what would change it.
