Lessons

The idea, the framework, a real example, where it breaks, and a quick drill.

1Symptom, problem, or solution?Most "problems" that reach a PM are one of the other two.8 min

The idea

Requests arrive in three disguises. A symptom is what you can see: a metric drops, tickets pile up, someone complains. A problem is the cause underneath, specific enough to work on. A solution in disguise is a fix stated as if it were the need: "we need a dashboard", "add live chat", "use AI". Your job is to move every request to the middle.

The framework

SymptomWhat we see
ProblemWhy it happens, for whom
SolutionsSeveral ways to fix it

Use 5 Whys, a technique from Toyota's production system: ask "why?" repeatedly, checking each answer against data, until you reach a cause you can act on. From a solution, go up instead: "what would that let you do?"

Real example

"Churn rose from 3% to 5%" is a symptom. Why? Churn is concentrated in teams that joined recently. Why? Most of them never invited a colleague. Why? A redesigned onboarding moved the invite step into settings. The problem isn't "churn"; it's a hidden invite step.

Where it breaks

  • One chain, one cause. Real problems often have several causes. Draw a tree when the "whys" branch.
  • "Why" can turn into blame. "Because Sam made a mistake" is never the root. Ask why the system let it happen.
  • Stories feel like answers. Check each "why" against data before you take the next step.

Drill: which is it?

"We need a mobile app."

"Average order value dropped 8% in October."

"New sellers can't upload more than 20 products at once, so big catalogues take days to list."

2Writing a problem statementOne sentence that a whole team can aim at.7 min

The idea

A good problem statement makes it obvious who is struggling, with what, why, and how you'll know it's fixed. It never contains the solution, so the team can still find a better one.

The framework

[Who] struggles to [do what] because [obstacle], which leads to [consequence, with evidence]. We'll know it's solved when [outcome].

  1. Specific who: "first-time sellers", not "users".
  2. No solution words: no "app", "dashboard", "AI", "button".
  3. Evidence: a number, a quote, a pattern you saw.
  4. Outcome: the behaviour change from M1.

Real example

Weak: "Users need better search." Strong: "First-time buyers on mobile struggle to find a product when they don't know its exact name, because search only matches exact titles, so 30% of mobile searches return nothing and those sessions end. We'll know it's solved when fewer than 10% of searches come back empty."

Where it breaks

  • Written backwards. A statement crafted to justify a solution already chosen. Tell-tale sign: the obstacle is "because we don't have [our feature]".
  • Too broad or too narrow. "People struggle to stay healthy" is a mission. "The save button is grey" is a task.

Pick the best problem statement.

3Jobs to be donePeople don't buy products. They hire them to make progress.9 min

The idea

Clayton Christensen's theory: when people buy something, they "hire" it to get a job done in a particular situation. Understand the job, and you understand both who your real competitors are and what "better" means.

The framework

When [situation], I want to [motivation], so I can [expected outcome].

This "job story" format focuses on the situation, not the person. Every job has three layers: functional (get it done), emotional (how I want to feel) and social (how I want to be seen).

Real example

Christensen's best-known story, told in Harvard Business Review: a fast-food chain wanted to sell more milkshakes. Asking customers about flavours went nowhere. Watching buyers showed that many bought one early in the morning, alone, for a long, boring commute. The shake was hired to make the drive less dull and keep them full until lunch. Its real competitors were bananas, bagels and boredom, not other milkshakes.

Where it breaks

  • Jobs written too abstractly. "Be happy" isn't a job you can design for.
  • It's a lens, not proof. A job story is still a hypothesis until real people confirm it.

4Issue trees and reframingBreak a big problem down, or look at it from a new side.8 min

The idea

Two tools for when a problem is too big or too stuck. An issue tree breaks it into smaller parts that together cover it fully, so nothing is missed. Reframing changes the question itself, which often reveals a cheaper fix.

The framework

  1. Issue tree: split the problem into branches that don't overlap and leave nothing out. Revenue = customers × revenue per customer. Customers = new + kept − lost. Then check which branch actually moved.
  2. Reframe: ask "what else could the problem be?" Look outside the frame, question the goal, look at the people involved, look for bright spots where it already works.

Real example

A classic reframing story, told by Thomas Wedell-Wedellsborg in Harvard Business Review: tenants complain that the lift is too slow. Framed as "the lift is slow", the fixes are expensive: faster motors, new algorithms. Reframed as "the wait is annoying", a cheap fix appears: put mirrors next to the lift. People check themselves out, and complaints drop.

Where it breaks

  • Trees built from guesses. A neat tree can still miss the real branch. Check branches with data.
  • Clever reframes can dodge the real issue. Mirrors are fine for a lift; they aren't fine if the lift is unsafe.

Drill

Subscription revenue is down 10%. Which first split covers the problem completely?

Users complain a report takes 40 seconds to load. Which is a reframe?

Cases

One team that found the real problem by going to it, one that built a solution and went looking for a problem.

Got it right

Airbnb's photos

In 2009, Airbnb was barely growing. The founders noticed that their New York listings had poor, dark phone photos. Instead of guessing at fixes from San Francisco, they flew to New York, rented a camera and photographed hosts' homes themselves. Bookings in the city rose quickly, and professional photography later became a programme.

Lesson: "growth is slow" was the symptom. "Guests can't trust what they can't see" was the problem, found by going to it.

Got it wrong

Google Glass

Google Glass launched to early "Explorers" in 2013 as a head-mounted computer. It was impressive technology without a clear everyday problem for consumers to solve, and it raised privacy worries for the people around the wearer. Google stopped selling the consumer Explorer edition in 2015 and later refocused Glass on workplace uses, where hands-free jobs were clear.

Lesson: a solution looking for a problem. It found a better fit once it was framed around specific jobs, like workers needing instructions with their hands full.

Apply it to your product

Take a request you received recently, or one from a product you know. Turn it into a problem statement and a job story. Your notes stay in this browser.

Show a worked example

Request: "Add a calendar view to the meeting-room booking app." A solution in disguise.

Problem statement: Team leads at a 300-person office struggle to find a free room for 6 to 8 people at short notice, because the app only lists rooms one at a time, so they book the wrong size or give up and meet in the kitchen. We'll know it's solved when most same-day bookings for groups take under a minute.

Job story: When my team's plans change that morning, I want to grab a room that fits us, so I can keep the meeting instead of moving it.

Now compare fixes: a calendar view, a "find a room for 8 now" button, or showing size filters first. The cheapest may win.

Teach it

Explaining an idea in your own words is the fastest way to find out whether you really understand it. Your answer stays in this browser.

A colleague brings you a request: "We need a mobile app." In three or four sentences, explain how you would turn it into a real problem before anyone builds anything.

Good explanations name the request as a solution in disguise, show the questions you would ask, and end with a one-sentence problem statement.

Mastery check

Five questions. They come back, mixed in, at later stations.

1. A stakeholder says "we need a dashboard". What's your best first question?

2. What's the biggest weakness of 5 Whys?

3. Which problem statement has a solution hiding in it?

4. Commuters "hire" a morning milkshake. What does jobs-to-be-done suggest you improve?

5. Tenants say the lift is too slow. Which response reframes the problem?

Key takeaways

  1. Move every request to the problem: from a symptom, ask why; from a solution, ask what it would let people do.
  2. Problem statements name who, what, why and the consequence, and never the fix.
  3. People hire products for jobs. Your real competitors are anything else hired for the same job.
  4. Stuck? Break it down with an issue tree, or reframe the question.
