Product discovery

Interviews that get the truth instead of compliments, mapping opportunities before solutions, finding your riskiest assumptions, and testing them cheaply.

Lessons

The idea, the framework, a real example, where it breaks, and a quick drill.

1Interviews that get the truthAsk about their life, in the past, specifically. Never about your idea.10 min

The idea

People are kind. Ask whether they like your idea and they'll say yes. Rob Fitzpatrick's The Mom Test is built on that: ask questions even your mum couldn't lie to you about. Facts about what people did, not opinions about what they might do.

The framework

  1. Talk about their life, not your idea. Don't pitch until the learning is done, if at all.
  2. Ask about specific past events, not general habits or the future. Teresa Torres calls this story-based interviewing: "Tell me about the last time you..."
  3. Dig into what they say: "What happened next? What did that cost you? What have you tried?"
  4. Listen more than you talk, and write down what they did, not what they said they'd do.

Weak questions to catch yourself on: "Would you use...?", "How much would you pay...?", "Don't you think...?", "What features do you want?"

Real example

"Would you use an app to track your hours?" gets "Sure!" "Tell me about the last time you invoiced a client" gets "I scrolled through my calendar for 20 minutes and probably forgot some hours." The second answer tells you where the pain is and roughly what it costs.

Where it breaks

  • Interviews find problems, not solutions. Users rarely know what to build; they know what hurts.
  • Small samples mislead. Look for patterns across several interviews, and back them with data where you can.
  • Memory is imperfect. Recent, specific events are more reliable than "usually".

Drill: good question or weak?

"Would you pay €10 a month for this?"

"What did you do the last time a client paid late?"

"Isn't it frustrating to plan shifts by hand?"

2Opportunity solution treesMap the problem space before you fall for a solution.9 min

The idea

Teresa Torres, in Continuous Discovery Habits, gives teams a simple visual: start from the outcome, map the opportunities (needs, pains, desires) you've heard in interviews, then generate several solutions per opportunity, and test each with small experiments. Read her overview.

The framework

OutcomeThe change you want (M1)
OpportunitiesNeeds and pains from interviews
SolutionsSeveral per opportunity
TestsSmall experiments
  1. Opportunities come from what customers said and did, not from what the team imagines.
  2. Pick one opportunity at a time, and compare at least three solutions for it.
  3. Test assumptions behind the solutions, not the whole solution at once.

Real example

Outcome: freelancers bill more of the hours they work. Opportunities from interviews: "I forget to record hours", "I don't know which client a task belongs to", "chasing late payers is awkward". For "I forget to record hours": a timer, automatic tracking from the calendar, or a Friday prompt to confirm the week. Three solutions to compare, not one to defend.

Where it breaks

  • It becomes a diagram for its own sake. The value is in the conversations it forces, not the drawing.
  • Opportunities phrased as solutions ("needs a timer") sneak back in. Write them as the customer's words.

Drill

Which is written as an opportunity, not a solution?

3Mapping your assumptionsFind the beliefs that matter most and that you know least about.7 min

The idea

Every solution rests on assumptions. Most teams test the ones that are easy to test. Instead, test the ones that would sink the idea if wrong and that you have the least evidence for. David Bland and Alex Osterwalder's Testing Business Ideas calls this assumption mapping.

The framework

  1. List assumptions under the four risks from M1: value, usability, feasibility, viability.
  2. Place each on two axes: how important is it to success, and how much evidence do we have?
  3. Test the top-left corner first: very important, little evidence. Those are your leap-of-faith assumptions.

Real example

A Friday "confirm your hours" prompt for freelancers. "Freelancers want to bill all their hours" is important but well evidenced. "They'll take two minutes every Friday to confirm" is important and unproven. Test that one first: send the prompt by email by hand to ten freelancers for three weeks and count replies.

Where it breaks

  • Scoring by gut. "Evidence" should mean things people did, not things they said.
  • Assumptions hide inside other assumptions. Keep asking "what would have to be true for this to work?"

Drill

Which assumption should you test first?

4Cheap testsLearn in days what building would teach you in months.9 min

The idea

Before building, fake it, do it by hand, or show it. The goal is the cheapest test that would change your decision.

The framework

Fake doorA button or page for something that doesn't exist yet. Measures real interest.
ConciergeDeliver the service by hand, openly, for a few customers.
Wizard of OzIt looks automated to the user; a person does the work behind it.
Prototype testA clickable mock-up with five target users. Tests usability.

Before running any test, write down what result would make you stop, change course or continue.

Real example

Before Dropbox had a product people could use, founder Drew Houston posted a short demo video showing how file syncing would work. The beta waiting list reportedly jumped from about 5,000 to 75,000 people overnight. Value tested, with almost no product built.

Where it breaks

  • Fake doors cost trust if used carelessly. Tell people honestly what happens next and keep it small.
  • Interest isn't usage. A sign-up proves curiosity; retention needs a real product.

Drill: which test fits?

You want to know if restaurants will value weekly rota planning, before writing any code.

You want to know if people can use a new checkout flow.

Cases

One team that tested demand before building, one that tested the wrong thing very thoroughly.

Got it right

Dropbox's demo video

Building reliable file syncing was hard and slow. Before it was ready, Drew Houston made a short video showing how it would work, aimed at early tech adopters. The beta waiting list reportedly grew from about 5,000 to 75,000 people overnight.

Lesson: the cheapest test of value was showing, not building.

Got it wrong

New Coke

In 1985, Coca-Cola replaced its formula after very large blind taste tests showed people preferred the sweeter new version. The tests measured taste in a sip. They didn't measure what the original meant to people. The backlash was so strong that the original returned as "Coca-Cola Classic" within three months.

Lesson: a careful test of the wrong question. Discovery has to find what people value, not just what they prefer in a lab.

Mastery check

Five questions, including one each from M1 and M4. They come back, mixed in, at later stations.

1. Which interview question gets the most reliable information?

2. In an opportunity solution tree, where do opportunities come from?

3. You think hotels would pay for an AI concierge. What's a sensible first test?

4. From M1: your test shows strong sign-ups but you're unsure people can complete setup. Which risk is next?

5. From M4: a fake-door test shows a "Coming soon" button. What keeps it honest?

Key takeaways

  1. Ask about their life, in the past, specifically. Never ask if they like your idea.
  2. Map opportunities before solutions, and compare several solutions for each.
  3. Test what's critical and unproven first, not what's easy to test.
  4. Fake it, do it by hand, or show it before you build it.
