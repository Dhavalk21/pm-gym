1Business modelsHow the value you create turns into money.8 min

The idea

A business model is how a product turns the value it creates into revenue, and whether that revenue covers the cost of delivering it. The same product can be paid for in very different ways, and the choice shapes everything from features to who you build for.

The framework

  1. Subscription: a recurring fee for ongoing access. Predictable, lives or dies on retention.
  2. Usage-based: pay for what you use, like API calls or storage. Fair, fits when costs grow with use.
  3. Transactions and marketplaces: a fee or a cut of each sale.
  4. Advertising: users get it free; advertisers pay for their attention.
  5. Freemium: free for most, paid for those who need more (M12).

Real example

Spotify runs two models side by side: a free tier paid for by advertising, and paid subscriptions without ads. The free tier is also the biggest source of future subscribers. The two models only work together because the free version is good enough to love and limited enough to upgrade from.

Where it breaks

  • Who pays isn't always who uses. In advertising, and often in business software, the buyer's needs can differ from the user's.
  • The model must match your costs. A flat price with heavy AI or bandwidth costs per user can lose money on your best customers.

2Pricing on valuePrice on what customers gain, not on what it cost you.9 min

The idea

There are three ways to set a price. Cost-plus adds a margin to your costs. Competitor-based copies the market. Value-based starts from what the product is worth to the customer. The first two are easy; the third usually captures far more, and is where good PMs start.

The framework

  1. Understand the value: time saved, money earned, risk avoided, compared with the customer's next best option.
  2. Pick a value metric: the unit your price grows with, like seats, invoices or minutes, which should grow as customers get more value.
  3. Research willingness to pay: for example the Van Westendorp questions: at what price is it too cheap to trust, a bargain, getting expensive, and too expensive to consider?
  4. Test and adjust: new customers first, with clear communication.

Real example

Slack charges per active user, and its fair billing policy credits companies for members who stop using it. Customers pay as value grows, and never for people who don't use it. That fairness reduces the fear of rolling it out to a whole company.

Where it breaks

  • Customers can't always say what they'd pay. Use survey answers as a range, and check against real behaviour.
  • Price changes for existing customers are risky. Explain why, give notice, and consider keeping old prices for a while.

3Packaging and tiersGood, better, best, and why the top tier exists.8 min

The idea

Packaging decides what goes into each plan. Most products use three tiers, often called good, better, best, so different customers can pay for what they need. The tiers also shape choices: people compare options against each other, not in isolation.

The framework

  1. Build tiers around customer types, not around leftover features: freelancer, small team, company.
  2. Fence the tiers with things that matter to the higher segment, like admin controls, security and support.
  3. Use anchoring honestly (M4): a premium tier makes the middle one look reasonable.
  4. Keep it simple: if customers need a call to understand your pricing page, it's too complicated.

Real example

Behavioural economist Dan Ariely tested an old Economist offer with students: web only for $59, print only for $125, or print and web for $125. With all three, 84% chose print and web. Without the print-only option, which almost nobody chose, most picked web only. The "useless" option changed the decision.

Where it breaks

  • Decoys can turn into dark patterns when they mislead. Anchoring should clarify value, not trick people.
  • Too many tiers or add-ons paralyse buyers. Three plans and a custom option is usually enough.

4Unit economicsDoes each customer earn more than they cost?9 min

The idea

Unit economics asks whether one customer is profitable. If they aren't, growth makes losses bigger, not smaller. A few numbers tell the story, and a PM should know them for their product.

The framework

  1. Customer acquisition cost (CAC): what it costs to win one customer.
  2. Gross profit per customer per month: revenue minus the direct cost to serve them.
  3. Lifetime value (LTV): monthly gross profit divided by monthly churn.
  4. Checks: LTV at least about three times CAC, and CAC earned back within roughly a year.

Real example

A customer pays €20 a month at a 75% gross margin, so €15 of gross profit. If 5% of customers leave each month, the average one stays about 20 months: LTV ≈ €15 ÷ 0.05 = €300. With a CAC of €90, LTV is over three times CAC, and the cost is earned back in six months. Healthy.

Where it breaks

  • LTV is an estimate, especially for young products with little churn history. Use cohorts (M10) and be conservative.
  • Blended averages hide bad channels. One ad channel can lose money on every customer while the average looks fine.

Drill: do the maths

€40 a month, 50% gross margin, 4% monthly churn, CAC €600. Is it healthy?

Cases

One company that changed its whole business model and grew, and one that changed its prices so abruptly that customers left in droves.

Got it right

Adobe's move to subscriptions

In 2013, Adobe stopped selling boxed Creative Suite software for a large one-off price and moved to Creative Cloud subscriptions. Revenue dipped during the switch and some customers protested. But subscriptions brought steady recurring revenue and continuous updates, and Adobe's annual revenue grew from about $4.4 billion in 2012 to over $11 billion by 2019.

Lesson: a business model change can be worth a painful transition, if it matches how customers get value over time.

Got it wrong

Netflix's 2011 price change

In July 2011, Netflix split its combined DVD and streaming plan into two separate plans, raising the price for customers who wanted both by 60%. Customers were angry about the price and the way it was announced. Netflix lost about 800,000 US subscribers in the following quarter, and its share price fell sharply.

Lesson: how you change prices matters as much as what you change them to. Explain the value, give notice, and avoid surprising loyal customers.

Key takeaways

  1. Pick a business model whose revenue grows with your costs and customers' value.
  2. Price on value, with a value metric customers understand and accept.
  3. Package tiers around customer types, and use anchoring to clarify, never to trick.
  4. Know your unit economics: lifetime value comfortably above acquisition cost, paid back within about a year.
