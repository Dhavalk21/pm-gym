Behavioural psychology

Why people act or don't, how habits form, the biases every product uses whether it means to or not, and where a nudge turns into a dark pattern.

A budgeting app: 70% of new users connect their bank account, but only 15% open the app again in their second week. The growth team wants to send three push notifications a day.

Show a model answer

1. Motivation was high at sign-up and fades. Checking a budget is effort (low ability), and nothing in the app gives a reason to come back at the right moment (no useful prompt). People also avoid looking at money when it feels bad, so opening the app can feel like a small loss.

2. Raise ability and fix the prompt instead: one useful message at a natural moment, like "Your salary arrived. Here's what's left for the month after bills." Make the first screen show one simple, reassuring number rather than a full report.

Lessons

The idea, the framework, a real example, where it breaks, and a quick drill.

1What makes people actMotivation, ability and a prompt, at the same moment.8 min

The idea

Stanford researcher BJ Fogg's model says a behaviour happens when three things meet at the same moment: enough motivation, enough ability (it's easy enough), and a prompt. When people don't act, one of the three is missing.

The framework

MotivationDo they want to, right now? It rises and falls, and it's hard to raise on purpose.
AbilityHow easy is it? Time, money, effort, thinking, habit. The most reliable lever.
PromptWhat reminds them, at the moment they're able and willing?

When something isn't happening, diagnose in this order: is there a prompt? Is it easy enough? Only then: are they motivated? Making a behaviour easier usually beats trying to motivate people more. See the model.

Real example

A fitness app sees people skip workouts. Pushing motivational quotes (motivation) does little. Cutting the default workout from 30 minutes to 7 (ability), and reminding people at the time they said they'd train (prompt), does far more.

Where it breaks

  • It explains a moment, not a habit. Getting someone to act once is different from getting them to keep acting.
  • Easy isn't always right. For important choices, like a big payment or deleting data, some friction protects people.

Drill: what's missing?

People want to export their data, and know it's possible, but it takes 12 steps. Most give up.

Users love the weekly report when they see it, but it's only available if they remember to open a menu.

2How habits formTrigger, action, reward, investment. And when not to bother.8 min

The idea

Some products are used because people decide to; others because it has become a habit. Nir Eyal's Hook Model describes the loop behind habit-forming products.

The framework

  1. Trigger: first external (a notification), later internal (boredom, worry, a feeling).
  2. Action: the simplest thing done in expectation of a reward.
  3. Variable reward: something useful or pleasing, not identical every time.
  4. Investment: the user puts something in (data, effort, progress) that makes the next round better and leaving costlier.

Real example

A language app: a reminder at your usual time (trigger), one short lesson (action), a mix of progress, praise and surprise (reward), and a streak and learning history you don't want to lose (investment). Duolingo has said publicly that streaks are one of its strongest drivers of daily use.

Where it breaks

  • Not every product should be a habit. Tax software, insurance, a mortgage: people want them to work well and rarely. Measure success there in jobs done, not visits.
  • Habit for whose benefit? If the loop helps the business but not the user, it's a compulsion. Eyal himself suggests asking whether you would use it, and whether it improves users' lives.

3Biases your product already usesDefaults, losses, anchors and other people.9 min

The idea

Every screen frames a choice. There is no neutral design: something is the default, something is shown first, something is compared to something else. Better to choose these on purpose.

The framework

DefaultsMost people keep what's pre-selected. Choose defaults that serve users.
Loss aversionLosses feel bigger than equal gains (Kahneman and Tversky). "Don't lose your progress" beats "keep going".
AnchoringThe first number seen shapes how others feel. Prices are judged against what's next to them.
Social proofPeople do what similar people do, especially when unsure.

Real example

Defaults are the strongest of all. A well-known 2003 study by Johnson and Goldstein in Science found organ donor consent was far higher in European countries where people were donors unless they opted out than in countries where they had to opt in. Same people, same choice, different default. In products, the pre-selected plan, the notification settings at install and the privacy toggle are all defaults doing the same work.

Where it breaks

  • Effects vary a lot by context. Some famous nudge results haven't held up when repeated. Treat a bias as a hypothesis and A/B test it.
  • Users notice. Repeated tricks erode trust, and trust is slow to rebuild.

Drill: which bias?

"You'll lose your 340 saved recipes if you cancel."

A wine list puts a €120 bottle at the top, so the €45 bottle feels reasonable.

4Nudge or sludge: ethics and dark patternsThe same lever can help people or work against them.8 min

The idea

A nudge makes it easier for people to do what they themselves want. Sludge, a term popularised by economist Richard Thaler, is friction that works against them. Designer Harry Brignull named the worst cases dark patterns: interfaces built to trick people.

The framework

  1. Whose goal does it serve? If only the company's, look harder.
  2. Would you show users exactly how it works? If you'd be embarrassed, it's sludge.
  3. Is leaving as easy as joining? Symmetry is a quick test for subscriptions.

Common dark patterns: hard-to-cancel subscriptions, "No thanks, I don't like saving money" decline links (confirmshaming), pre-ticked add-ons, fake scarcity and countdowns, and hidden costs that appear at checkout.

Real example

The law is catching up. The EU's Digital Services Act bans online platforms from designing interfaces that deceive or manipulate users. In the US, the Federal Trade Commission sued Amazon in 2023 over Prime sign-up and cancellation flows it said were designed to trap people, and Amazon agreed in 2025 to pay $2.5 billion to settle.

Where it breaks

  • The line can be grey. Offering a pause or a discount before someone cancels is fine, as long as cancelling stays equally easy.
  • Short-term metrics reward sludge. Retention looks better this quarter. Add guardrail metrics: complaints, refunds, reviews.

Drill

Which retention flow is acceptable?

Cases

One habit loop built around the learner's own goal, one sign-up and cancel flow that ended in court.

Got it right

Duolingo streaks

Duolingo built its daily habit around a streak: a count of consecutive days practised, with reminders and "streak freezes" that let you miss a day without losing it. The company has said publicly that streaks are among its biggest drivers of daily engagement.

Lesson: loss aversion and a small daily action, pointed at something learners already want. The freeze matters: it eases the anxiety the streak itself creates.

Got it wrong

Amazon Prime cancellation

In 2023 the US Federal Trade Commission sued Amazon, alleging that Prime sign-up flows enrolled people without clear consent and that cancelling meant getting through a long, confusing sequence of pages. In 2025 Amazon agreed to pay $2.5 billion to settle the case.

Lesson: sludge can lift a retention chart for years, and then cost far more than it earned, in money and trust.

Game: Nudge or Sludge

Ten real-looking designs. Decide whether each helps users or works against them, then name the lever. Open it on its own page to share.

Apply it to your product

Pick one behaviour you want more of in a product you know. Diagnose it and design an honest nudge. Your notes stay in this browser.

Show a worked example

Product: a team task app. Behaviour: team members update their task status before the Monday meeting. Motivation is fine; ability is low (status is buried in a side panel) and there's no prompt.

Nudge: on Monday at 9:00, a message lists each person's three open tasks with one-tap status buttons (prompt and ability). Show "6 of 8 teammates have updated" (social proof, true numbers only).

Guardrail: watch how many people mute the message and whether updates are real or just tapped through. If mutes rise, cut the frequency.

Teach it

Explaining an idea in your own words is the fastest way to find out whether you really understand it. Your answer stays in this browser.

A colleague proposes adding "Only 3 left!" to every product page because it lifted conversion in a competitor's test. In three or four sentences, explain how you'd respond.

Good explanations name the lever, separate true scarcity from fake scarcity, and suggest a guardrail metric.

Key takeaways

  1. Behaviour needs motivation, ability and a prompt at the same moment. Make it easier before you try to motivate.
  2. Build habits only where frequent use is the user's own goal.
  3. There's no neutral screen. Choose defaults, anchors and framing on purpose.
  4. Nudges serve users; sludge works against them. If you wouldn't show users how it works, don't ship it.
