1What a language model actually doesIt predicts the next piece of text. Everything else follows from that.9 min

The idea

A large language model (LLM) is trained on enormous amounts of text to predict what comes next. It reads text as tokens, chunks of words, and generates an answer one token at a time, each time picking a likely next token. Further training with human feedback makes it follow instructions and behave helpfully.

Product managers ship outcomes, not outputs.

A rough rule of thumb for English: one token is about three-quarters of a word. Models are priced and limited in tokens.

The framework: four properties PMs must design for

  1. Probabilistic: the same question can get different answers. Plan for variation, not one fixed output.
  2. Plausible, not verified: it can state wrong things confidently ("hallucinations"). It has no built-in fact-check.
  3. Frozen knowledge: it knows only what was in its training data, up to a cut-off date, unless you give it more.
  4. Cost and speed: longer inputs and outputs, and bigger models, cost more and respond more slowly.

Real example

Ask a general model "What's our refund window?" and it will answer, because answering is what it predicts. It has never seen your policy, so it produces a typical-sounding period. That's not a bug you can fix with a better question; it's how the technology works. The fix is to give it the facts (lessons 2 and 3).

Where it breaks

  • "It's just autocomplete" undersells it. Predicting text well requires capturing a lot of structure: models can reason through problems, write code and follow complex instructions.
  • Precise maths and exact lookups are still better done by code the model calls, not by the model itself.

2Context is everythingThe model only knows what's in front of it. Deciding what goes there is the job.9 min

The idea

Every time a model answers, it sees one block of text: its context. Whatever isn't in there, it can't use. The context window has a size limit, and every token in it costs money and time. So product quality depends heavily on what you choose to put in.

The framework: what goes into the context

InstructionsRole, task, rules, tone, what to do when unsure
ExamplesA few good input and output pairs
FactsRetrieved documents, user data, tool results
ConversationWhat the user and model said so far
Output formatExactly the shape you need back

Prompting is writing the instructions and examples well. Context engineering is the wider job: deciding, for every request, which facts, history and tools to include, and what to leave out. A good practical guide to prompting.

Real example

A support assistant answers vaguely. Instead of a cleverer prompt, the team adds the customer's plan, their last three tickets and the two most relevant help articles to the context, plus the instruction "If the articles don't answer it, say so and offer a human." Answers become specific and the "I don't know" cases become visible.

Where it breaks

  • More isn't better. Stuffing in everything adds cost and delay, and irrelevant text can distract the model from what matters.
  • Prompt tweaks don't fix missing facts. If the information isn't in the context, no wording will produce it reliably.

3Grounding AI in your own dataEmbeddings and retrieval (RAG), in plain words.9 min

The idea

Your documents won't fit in one context, and the model wasn't trained on them. Retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) solves this: find the few passages relevant to each question, put them in the context, and have the model answer from them.

The framework

1. PrepareSplit documents into chunks; turn each into an embedding
2. RetrieveFind the chunks closest in meaning to the question
3. GenerateAnswer from those chunks, citing them

An embedding is a list of numbers that represents the meaning of a piece of text. Texts with similar meanings get similar numbers, so "How do I send something back?" lands close to a passage about returns, even with no words in common.

Real example

In 2023, Morgan Stanley's wealth management division rolled out an internal assistant built on OpenAI's GPT-4. It let financial advisors ask questions in plain language and get answers drawn from the firm's large library of research and internal documents, instead of searching through them by hand.

Where it breaks

  • Bad retrieval, confident answers. If the wrong chunks come back, the model answers well from the wrong facts. Most RAG failures are retrieval failures.
  • Stale or messy sources. RAG is only as good as the documents behind it. Outdated pages produce outdated answers.

4When to use AI, and when notThe cost of a wrong answer decides more than the wow factor.9 min

The idea

AI is a great fit for some problems and a poor one for others. The best AI PMs say no to AI often. Start from the problem (M2), then check whether a model is the right tool.

The framework: the AI fit check

  1. Is the input messy? Free text, images, varied phrasing: good for AI. Clean, structured data: often better with code.
  2. What does a wrong answer cost? Cheap or easily fixed: fine. Legal, financial, medical or irreversible: keep a human in the loop, or don't use a model for the decision.
  3. Can someone check the output cheaply? If yes, AI can draft and people approve.
  4. Would a simple rule do? If so, use the rule. It's cheaper, faster and always the same.
  5. Which model? Bigger models are better at hard tasks but cost more and are slower. Test smaller ones first on your real examples.

Real example

An invoicing product: generating a friendly payment reminder in the customer's language is a good fit (messy, low risk, easy to review). Calculating the VAT amount is not: it's exact, legally binding, and a few lines of ordinary code get it right every time.

Where it breaks

  • "Add AI" as a goal is a solution in disguise (M2). The question is always which problem it solves better than the alternatives.
  • Rules can get messy too. Hundreds of brittle rules for handling free text may be worse than one well-tested model.

Cases

One team that grounded AI in its own knowledge, one launch where a confident error cost dearly.

Got it right

Morgan Stanley's advisor assistant

Morgan Stanley's financial advisors had a huge library of research and internal documents that was slow to search. In 2023 the firm launched an assistant on OpenAI's GPT-4 that answered advisors' questions from that library. It was internal, so experts reviewed what it said before anything reached a client.

Lesson: a clear job, grounded in the firm's own documents, with expert users checking the output.

Got it wrong

Google Bard's launch demo

In February 2023, Google's promotional demo of its new chatbot Bard included an answer claiming the James Webb Space Telescope took the very first pictures of a planet outside our solar system. Astronomers quickly pointed out that was wrong. Alphabet's share price fell sharply the following day, wiping roughly $100 billion off its market value.

Lesson: plausible isn't true. Even a carefully prepared demo needs its facts checked, because the model can't do that for you.

Show a worked example

Idea: in a project tool, draft the weekly status update for each project. Problem: project leads spend about an hour every Friday writing updates nobody enjoys writing.

Fit check: input is messy (task comments, changes, chat). A wrong sentence is cheap, because the lead reviews and edits before sending. No rule could do this. Good fit, with a human approving.

Context: instructions (audience, length, tone, "flag risks first", "never invent dates"), two example updates the team liked, this week's task changes and comments as facts, and an output format with headings for done, next and risks.

Teach it

Explaining an idea in your own words is the fastest way to find out whether you really understand it. Your answer stays in this browser.

A stakeholder asks: "Why can't we just plug ChatGPT into our website and let it answer customers?" In three or four sentences, explain what would go wrong and what you'd do instead.

Good explanations mention that the model doesn't know your facts, describe grounding it in your own content, and name the questions a person should still handle.

Key takeaways

  1. A model predicts plausible text. Design for variation and confident mistakes.
  2. It only knows what's in its context. Choosing what goes in is the product work.
  3. Ground answers in your own data with retrieval, and check what was retrieved when it goes wrong.
  4. Use AI where input is messy and mistakes are cheap or checked. Use code where answers must be exact.
