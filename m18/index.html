Zone 6, Ship and lead

Communication and influence

Writing that leads with the answer, telling stories with data, managing stakeholders so nobody is surprised, and influencing people you don't manage.

Lessons

Each one runs the same way: the idea, the framework, a real example, where it breaks, and a quick drill.

1Write it down, answer firstClear writing is clear thinking.8 min

The idea

Much of a PM's influence travels in writing: updates, memos, decision documents, messages. Busy readers decide in the first few lines whether to keep reading, so put the answer first: what's happening, why it matters to them, and what you need. Details follow for anyone who wants them.

The framework

  1. Lead with the point (sometimes called BLUF, bottom line up front): the decision, change or ask.
  2. Then the why: the two or three reasons that matter most to this reader.
  3. Then the details, grouped and ordered, for those who want them.
  4. End with the next step: who does what, by when.

Real example

In 2004, Jeff Bezos stopped slide presentations in Amazon's senior meetings and replaced them with narrative memos of up to six pages, read in silence at the start of each meeting. His reasoning: writing a good memo forces better thinking about what matters most and how ideas connect, which bullet points let you skip.

Where it breaks

  • Not everything needs a memo. Match the format to the decision: a chat message for small things, a document for big ones.
  • Answer-first can feel blunt. Add a sentence of context when the news is hard, but don't bury it.

2Stories with dataOne chart, one message, and the so-what.9 min

The idea

Data persuades when it answers a question the audience cares about, simply. A good chart has one message, written as its title; the right comparison (against last month, a target, another group); and a clear so-what: what we should do because of it.

The framework

  1. Start with the question the audience has, not with the data you have.
  2. Title charts with the message: "Late payments fell by a third after reminders launched", not "Payment data Q2".
  3. Show the comparison that makes the number meaningful.
  4. Pair numbers with a story: one real customer makes a statistic feel real.

Real example

In 1858, Florence Nightingale used a new kind of chart, now called her rose diagram, to show that most British soldiers who died in the Crimean War died of preventable disease, not battle wounds. The chart made one message impossible to miss, and helped win support for reforms in hospital sanitation.

Where it breaks

  • A clear story can mislead. Don't cut the axis or hide inconvenient data to make the point stronger.
  • Some audiences want the detail. Keep the full analysis available for those who'll check it.

3StakeholdersKnow who needs what, and never let them be surprised.8 min

The idea

A stakeholder is anyone affected by your work or able to affect it. A simple map, by power (how much they can influence the outcome) and interest (how much they care), helps decide how to work with each: manage closely, keep satisfied, keep informed, or just monitor.

The framework

  1. Map them: high power and high interest people get the most attention.
  2. Learn what each one cares about: revenue, risk, workload, customers, reputation.
  3. No surprises: tell people bad news before they hear it elsewhere.
  4. Talk one-to-one before big meetings, so meetings confirm decisions rather than start fights.

Real example

A launch slips six weeks. The head of sales, who has promised it to prospects, hears first, in person, with a draft message for prospects. The CFO gets a two-line note on revenue impact. Support gets a reply template. Engineering gets the technical reason. Same news, four messages, nobody surprised.

Where it breaks

  • Maps go stale. People change roles and priorities; revisit the map each quarter.
  • Don't ignore low-power, high-interest groups. Support teams and customers often see problems first.

4Influence without authorityYou lead people you don't manage. Trust is the currency.9 min

The idea

Most PMs don't manage the engineers, designers, marketers or salespeople they depend on. Influence comes from trust: being credible (you know the customer and the data), reliable (you do what you say) and fair (you represent other people's concerns honestly).

The framework

  1. Listen first: understand their goals, pressures and what they see that you don't.
  2. Find the shared goal: frame decisions around outcomes you both want.
  3. Bring evidence: customer stories and data persuade better than opinions (M5, M10).
  4. Propose small tests when you disagree, and once it's decided, commit fully.

Real example

Amazon's leadership principles include "disagree and commit". In his 2016 letter to shareholders, Jeff Bezos described approving an Amazon Studios project he wasn't convinced about, telling the team he disagreed but would commit to it. Healthy teams argue openly, then back the decision fully, instead of quietly undermining it.

Where it breaks

  • Influence isn't manipulation. If you'd be uncomfortable explaining your tactics to the person, don't use them.
  • Sometimes you need to escalate. Do it together and openly, after trying to resolve it directly, not behind someone's back.

Cases

One company that replaced slides with written narratives, and one investigation that found a life-threatening risk buried in a slide.

Got it right

Amazon's six-page memos

On 9 June 2004, Jeff Bezos told Amazon's senior team that slide presentations were out and written narratives were in. Meetings start with everyone silently reading a memo of up to six pages, then discussing it. Bezos argued that writing a good memo is harder than making slides, because it forces you to show what matters most and how ideas connect.

Lesson: clear writing exposes fuzzy thinking. Communicate in full sentences when the decision matters.

Got it wrong

The Columbia shuttle briefing

In January 2003, while Columbia was in orbit, engineers briefed NASA managers on a foam strike to the shuttle's wing using dense slides. A key fact, that the foam was hundreds of times larger than anything tested, sat at the bottom of a slide with a reassuring title. The accident board later criticised NASA's endemic use of slides instead of technical papers. Columbia broke apart on re-entry.

Lesson: put the most important message first and make risks impossible to miss. Format can hide what matters most.

Key takeaways

  1. Write answer first: the point, why it matters to the reader, what you need, what's next.
  2. One chart, one message, with the right comparison and a clear so-what.
  3. Map stakeholders by power and interest, tailor the same news to each, and never surprise them.
  4. Influence through trust: listen, find the shared goal, bring evidence, then disagree and commit.
