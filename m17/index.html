Lessons

Each one runs the same way: the idea, the framework, a real example, where it breaks, and a quick drill.

1Specs that help, not hinderA short document that answers why, for whom and what success looks like.8 min

The idea

A spec, often called a PRD or one-pager, isn't a contract that tells engineers exactly what to build. It's a shared understanding of the problem, the goal and the boundaries, so the team can make good decisions without you in every conversation.

The framework

  1. Problem and evidence: who has it, how we know, how big it is.
  2. Goal and success metric: what will change, and how we'll measure it (M9).
  3. Scope and non-goals: what's in, and what we're deliberately leaving out.
  4. Open questions and risks: what we don't know yet, and how we'll find out.
  5. Keep it alive: update it as you learn; link designs and decisions.

Real example

Amazon starts many new products by writing the press release and a list of frequently asked questions before building anything, a process described in Working Backwards by former executives Colin Bryar and Bill Carr. If the press release isn't exciting for customers, the product probably isn't either, and it's much cheaper to rewrite a document than a product.

Where it breaks

  • Long specs don't get read. If it's more than a few pages, split it or cut it.
  • Specs can't replace conversations. The best ones are written with design and engineering, not handed to them.

Drill: what's missing?

A spec lists 30 detailed requirements, but no problem statement and no success metric. What's the risk?

2Working with design and engineeringOne team, thin slices, shared ownership.8 min

The idea

The best product work happens when a PM, a designer and an engineering lead work as one small group from the start, often called the product trio. Engineers who understand the problem suggest simpler solutions; designers who hear the constraints design what can actually ship.

The framework

  1. Involve engineering and design in discovery (M5), not only in delivery.
  2. Slice work thin: small pieces that each deliver something usable, end to end, instead of finishing layer by layer.
  3. Agree what "done" means: tested, measured, documented, released.
  4. Protect focus: limit work in progress, and say no to mid-sprint surprises unless they're urgent.

Real example

Instead of building the whole new invoice editor and releasing it in three months, the team ships one slice every two weeks: first a new line-item table, then templates, then the new export menu. Each slice reaches real customers behind a flag, so problems show up while they're still small.

Where it breaks

  • Some work can't be sliced thin, like a database migration. Then reduce risk with testing, dry runs and a rollback plan.
  • Over-collaboration slows teams down. Not every decision needs a meeting; agree who decides what.

3Launching safelyRoll out gradually, watch closely, keep an off switch.9 min

The idea

A launch is a risk as well as a moment. Modern teams separate deploying code from releasing it to customers, using feature flags that turn features on for chosen groups. That allows a staged rollout: a small share of users first, then more, while watching for problems.

The framework

  1. Roll out in stages: internal team, 1%, 10%, 50%, 100%, with checks between each.
  2. Watch the right signals: errors, speed, the success metric and guardrails.
  3. Keep an off switch: know how to roll back in minutes, and who decides.
  4. Prepare people: support briefed, help articles ready, sales and marketing timed to the rollout.

Real example

On 19 July 2024, a faulty configuration update from the security company CrowdStrike was live for 78 minutes before being withdrawn, long enough to crash millions of Windows computers, grounding flights and disrupting hospitals and banks. Microsoft estimated 8.5 million devices were affected. CrowdStrike's response included moving to staged rollouts for these updates, starting with a small "canary" group.

Where it breaks

  • Not every change needs a staged rollout. Small, low-risk changes can go faster; match the process to the risk.
  • Flags pile up. Remove them once a feature is fully launched, or they become their own tech debt (M8).

4After launchMeasure honestly, review without blame, decide what's next.8 min

The idea

Launch isn't the finish line; it's when you start learning what you actually built. Check the agreed success metric after the agreed time, look at guardrails, listen to support and users, then decide: keep going, improve, or roll back.

The framework

  1. Measure against the goal you set in the spec, after a fair window, not on day one.
  2. Hold a blameless review: what happened, why, what we learned, what we'll change in the system.
  3. Share the results, good or bad, with the people who care.
  4. Decide the next step: iterate, scale up, or stop and remove it.

Real example

Google's site reliability engineering teams popularised the blameless postmortem: after an incident, the review looks for gaps in systems and processes, not for someone to blame. People share honestly what happened when they aren't afraid of punishment, which is the only way to fix the real causes.

Where it breaks

  • Teams rush to the next project and skip the review. Put it in the calendar before launch.
  • Blameless isn't consequence-free. It means fixing systems first; repeated carelessness is still a management conversation.

Cases

One company that writes the press release before building, and one update that reached millions of computers at once.

Got it right

Amazon's press release first

Amazon teams often start a new product by writing its press release and a list of hard questions and answers, before any code. Former executives Colin Bryar and Bill Carr describe how the process shaped products such as Kindle: the team kept rewriting the release until it described something customers would truly want.

Lesson: being clear on the customer, the problem and what success looks like, before building, saves far more time than it costs.

Got it wrong

The CrowdStrike outage

On 19 July 2024, a faulty configuration update from CrowdStrike, a security company, was sent to customers' Windows computers and made them crash on start-up. Microsoft estimated 8.5 million devices were affected, disrupting airlines, hospitals and banks. CrowdStrike said it would deploy such updates gradually, starting with a small canary group, and give customers more control over timing.

Lesson: anything that reaches many users at once needs a staged rollout and a fast way back, however routine the change seems.

Key takeaways

  1. Write short specs about the why: problem, goal, metric, scope and non-goals.
  2. Work as one trio and ship thin slices, so problems appear while they're small.
  3. Launch behind a flag: stage the rollout, watch the signals, keep an off switch, prepare support.
  4. After launch, measure on the agreed window and review without blame.
