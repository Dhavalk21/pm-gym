Zone 3, Measure

Metrics and North Star

What makes a metric useful, choosing a North Star, breaking it into inputs teams can move, and the guardrails that stop targets being gamed.

Lessons

1What makes a good metricRates over totals, leading over lagging, and no vanity.8 min

The idea

A metric is only useful if it helps you decide something. A good one is understandable (anyone can say what it means), comparable (this week against last week, one group against another), and tells you what to do when it moves. A vanity metric looks impressive but changes no decision.

The framework

  1. Prefer rates and ratios to totals. "Share of sign-ups who send a first invoice" beats "total sign-ups".
  2. Know leading from lagging. Revenue and churn lag: they report the past. Activation and weekly usage lead: they predict it.
  3. Ask the decision test: if this number doubled or halved tomorrow, what would we do differently? No answer means vanity.

Real example

"Total registered users" only ever goes up, so every board slide looks like success. The same company's "share of new users still active in week four" might be falling, which is the number that tells you the product is getting worse at keeping people.

Where it breaks

  • Not everything that matters can be counted. Trust, brand and team health are real even when the dashboard can't show them.
  • Ratios can hide size. A conversion rate that doubles on ten users is noise. Look at the counts behind every rate.

2The North Star metricOne number that captures the value customers get.9 min

The idea

A North Star metric is the single number that best captures the value your product delivers to customers, and that leads to sustainable revenue. It gives every team the same direction: if your work doesn't eventually move it, ask why you're doing it. The idea was popularised by growth leader Sean Ellis and later turned into a playbook by Amplitude.

The framework

  1. It reflects customer value, not just business value. Revenue is a result; the North Star is the reason people pay.
  2. It leads revenue: when it rises, money follows later.
  3. It's measurable often, ideally weekly, so teams see progress.
  4. It's one number, with a short list of inputs underneath (next lesson).

Real example

Airbnb reports "nights and experiences booked" as a headline number to investors. It counts value for both sides at once: a guest got a stay and a host got paid. A team that improves search, trust or host onboarding can trace its work to it.

Where it breaks

  • Some businesses need two: a marketplace can watch supply and demand separately, and a company with very different products may need one per product.
  • A North Star can still be gamed or hide harm on its own. That's why it needs guardrails (lesson 4).

3Metric trees and input metricsBreak the big number into parts teams can move.9 min

The idea

Nobody can move a North Star directly. A metric tree breaks it into input metrics: smaller numbers a team can actually influence, which add up to the big one. Amazon calls them controllable input metrics and reviews them every week.

The framework

North StarNights booked
= SearchersBreadth
× Search-to-book rateConversion
× Nights per bookingDepth

Common ways to split: breadth (how many people), depth (how much each one gets), frequency (how often) and efficiency (how easily). Give each input an owner.

Real example

In Working Backwards, former Amazon executives Colin Bryar and Bill Carr describe choosing "number of new detail pages" as an input for selection. Teams added thousands of products, many rarely viewed or out of stock, and sales didn't rise. The metric evolved into the share of product-page views where the item was in stock and ready for fast shipping, which did move sales.

Where it breaks

  • A tree shows math, not cause. Check that pushing an input actually moves the output, as Amazon did; drop inputs that don't.
  • Too many inputs and nobody knows what matters. Three to five per North Star is plenty.

4Guardrails and Goodhart's lawEvery target gets gamed. Plan for it.8 min

The idea

Economist Charles Goodhart's observation, in anthropologist Marilyn Strathern's popular phrasing: when a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure. People find the cheapest way to move a number, which isn't always the way you meant. A guardrail (or counter-metric) is a number that must not get worse while you push your target.

The framework

  1. Pair every target with a guardrail: speed with quality, growth with retention, engagement with opt-outs or complaints.
  2. Ask "how would I cheat?" before setting a target. If the answer is easy, someone will find it.
  3. Watch the counts and the stories, not just the number. Talk to the people behind it.

Real example

A support team targeted on "tickets closed per hour" starts closing tickets before problems are solved. The fix isn't a new target; it's a guardrail: "tickets reopened within 7 days". Speed now only counts if it sticks.

Where it breaks

  • Too many guardrails freeze a team. Pick the one or two harms that are both likely and serious.
  • Guardrails need thresholds agreed in advance ("no more than a 2% rise"), or every review turns into a debate.

Cases

One company that kept refining its inputs until they drove the result, and one bank whose target broke the business.

Got it right

Amazon's input metrics

Amazon reviews a deck of controllable input metrics every week. When "new detail pages" didn't lead to more sales, teams refined it step by step until it measured what customers actually experienced: the share of product views where the item was in stock and ready to ship fast. Driving that number did move sales.

Lesson: start with an imperfect input, check it moves the output, and keep refining.

Got it wrong

Wells Fargo's sales targets

Wells Fargo pushed branch staff hard to sell more products per customer. Employees opened millions of accounts customers had never asked for. In 2016 the bank paid $185 million in fines, and the scandal cost it billions more and years of trust.

Lesson: a target with no guardrail on customer harm will eventually be met in the cheapest, worst way.

Key takeaways

  1. A good metric changes a decision. Prefer rates to totals, and leading to lagging.
  2. The North Star counts the value customers get, and revenue follows it.
  3. Break it into a few inputs teams can own, and check they really move it.
  4. Every target gets gamed. Pair it with a guardrail before you start.
