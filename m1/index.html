Zone 1, Understand

Product thinking basics

Outcomes over outputs, the four risks every idea carries, which decisions you can take back, and what happens after the first effect.

Lessons

Each one runs the same way: the idea, the framework, a real example, where it breaks, and a quick drill. Open one at a time.

1Outcomes, not outputsWhat you ship is not what you're paid for.8 min

The idea

An output is something the team makes: a feature, a redesign, a launch. An outcome is a change in what people do because of it. An impact is the business result that follows. Teams that only track outputs can ship everything on the roadmap and still change nothing.

The framework

OutputWhat we ship
OutcomeWhat people do differently
ImpactWhat the business gets

Write an outcome as: who does what differently, measured how. Then ask: if we ship this and nobody's behaviour changes, did we succeed? If the honest answer is yes, you are tracking an output.

Real example

The meal-kit CEO asked for AI meal plans (output). The outcome could be "subscribers skip fewer weeks" or "new subscribers keep ordering past their second month". The impact is lower churn and more revenue. Once the outcome is clear, AI plans become one option among several: better recipes, a simpler skip flow, or reminders might move it more cheaply.

Where it breaks

  • Outcomes lag. Retention takes months to show. Track a leading signal that moves early, like plans accepted in week one.
  • Some work really is output. A legal deadline or a contractual commitment just has to ship.
  • You don't control outcomes alone. Seasons, prices and competitors move them too. Judge teams on learning, not just on the number.

Drill: output, outcome or impact?

"Launch saved searches by June"

"New users place their first order within 3 days of signing up"

"Annual revenue grows by 15%"

"Subscribers skip 20% fewer weeks"

2The four product risksEvery idea can die four ways. Test the likeliest first.10 min

The idea

Marty Cagan of SVPG groups what can go wrong with any product idea into four risks. Good teams name them before building, and test the one most likely to kill the idea first, as cheaply as possible.

The framework

ValueWill people want it, choose it, pay for it?
UsabilityCan people figure out how to use it?
FeasibilityCan we build it with the time, skills and tech we have?
ViabilityDoes it work for the business: legal, finance, sales, brand, support?
  1. List your assumptions under each risk.
  2. For each, ask: how likely is it wrong, and how bad if it is?
  3. Test the worst one first, with the cheapest test that gives a real answer: a fake door or pre-order page for value, five-person prototype tests for usability, an engineering spike for feasibility, a review with legal or finance for viability.

Read Cagan's original short article

Real example

AI meal plans. Value: do subscribers want plans, or enjoy choosing? Usability: will they trust and edit them? Feasibility: can the AI respect allergies every single time? Viability: an allergen mistake is a safety and liability issue, and each plan costs money to generate. Value is the likeliest killer, and it can be tested without any AI at all.

Where it breaks

  • Teams test what they're comfortable with. Engineers love proving feasibility; it's rarely the risk that kills consumer ideas.
  • Small, reversible changes don't need a risk workshop. Ship behind a switch and measure.
  • The risks overlap. With AI products, output quality touches value, usability and viability at once.

Drill: which risk is this assumption about?

"Our legal team will approve storing health data in this region."

"Small agencies will pay €49 a month for automated reports."

"First-time users will find the export button without help."

3One-way and two-way doorsMatch your speed to how reversible the decision is.6 min

The idea

In his 2015 letter to shareholders, Jeff Bezos split decisions into two types. One-way doors are hard or impossible to reverse, so make them slowly and carefully. Two-way doors can be walked back through, so make them fast, with small groups and less process. His warning: big companies tend to treat every decision as a one-way door, and slow to a crawl.

The framework

  1. Can we undo it? And what would undoing cost: money, trust, data, time?
  2. What does waiting cost? A slow decision has a price too.
  3. Can we turn a one-way door into a two-way one? A pilot, a feature switch, a small market or a time limit often can.

Real example

Testing a new onboarding flow on 5% of new users behind a switch: two-way, decide this week. Raising prices for every existing customer by email: much closer to one-way, because trust and expectations don't reset when you change your mind. Test the price on a slice of new customers first.

Where it breaks

  • Two-way doors harden. Once customers rely on a feature, or data has migrated, "we can always roll back" stops being true.
  • Calling everything reversible is an excuse to skip thinking. Be honest about what undoing really costs.

Drill: one-way or two-way?

Changing a button label for 10% of users in an A/B test.

Deleting old user data to cut storage costs.

Publicly committing to a launch date at a big industry conference.

4Second-order thinkingAsk "and then what?" one more time than feels natural.7 min

The idea

First-order thinking stops at the immediate effect: a discount brings sign-ups. Second-order thinking asks what that effect causes next: who signs up, what they expect, how competitors react. Most product surprises live in the second step.

The framework

  1. Effect chain: write the first effect, then ask "and then what?" twice.
  2. Who else? Check each group: users, the team, support, finance, partners, competitors.
  3. Over time: how does it look in 10 days, 10 months and 2 years?

Real example

A meal-kit app offers 60% off the first box. First order: sign-ups jump. Second order: many new customers came for the deal, so more of them cancel after the first box; people learn to wait for discounts; support handles more cancellations. Third order: competitors match the offer and acquisition gets more expensive for everyone. The sign-up chart looked great the whole time.

Where it breaks

  • Analysis paralysis. You can't trace every effect. Go deep on one-way doors; for two-way doors, ship and watch.
  • Confident chains are still guesses. Turn the scariest second-order effect into a metric you watch, like retention of discounted customers.

Drill: what's the likeliest second-order effect?

A team makes a free trial 90 days instead of 14 to boost sign-ups.

Sales gets a bonus for every new contract signed, regardless of size or fit.

Cases

One team that followed the outcome, one that solved the wrong risk.

Got it right

Instagram, from Burbn

In 2010, Kevin Systrom and Mike Krieger were building Burbn, a location check-in app with plans, points and photos. Usage showed people mostly cared about one thing: sharing photos. They cut almost everything else, rebuilt around photos and filters, and relaunched as Instagram.

Lesson: they followed what users actually did (the outcome), not the product they had planned (the output).

Got it wrong

Juicero

Juicero launched a Wi-Fi connected juice press in 2016 for $699, later cut to $399, selling juice packs by subscription. In 2017, Bloomberg showed the packs could be squeezed by hand about as well as the machine. The company shut down that same year.

Lesson: the engineering (feasibility) was impressive. The value question, why anyone needs the machine, was never answered.

Mastery check

Five questions. They come back, mixed in, at later stations.

1. A team ships every roadmap feature on time, but retention doesn't move. Which risk did they most likely skip?

2. Which of these is written as an outcome?

3. You want to raise prices for all existing customers, announced by email. How should you treat it?

4. A 50% first-order discount doubles sign-ups. What's the likeliest second-order effect?

5. Your CEO wants a feature because a competitor launched it. What's your first move?

Key takeaways

  1. Outputs are what you ship; outcomes are what people do differently. Write goals as outcomes.
  2. Every idea carries value, usability, feasibility and viability risk. Test the likeliest killer first, cheaply.
  3. Match speed to reversibility. Fast on two-way doors; careful on one-way doors, and turn them into two-way ones where you can.
  4. Ask "and then what?" twice. The surprises are in the second step.
